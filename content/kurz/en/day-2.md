Yesterday we said you brief a task, not a topic. Today let's look at what a good brief is made of — and what to do with an answer that isn't quite right.

## Today's idea

A prompt that returns usable work has four parts. No technique, no secret syntax — it's simply the way you'd brief a task to a person.

**Role** sets the perspective and the vocabulary. “You're a sales director looking for holes in a proposal” returns something different from “you're a copywriter.”

**Context** is the one thing the model doesn't know. Who you are, what happened, who the output is for, and what that person will do with it. Don't be afraid to write a lot — until you tell it, it's guessing.

**Task** is one verb and a clear scope. Not “think about,” but “list,” “rewrite,” “compare,” “propose three options.”

**Format** decides whether the output is actually usable. A table with three columns, a six-sentence email, bullet points with no intro.

And the second half of today's idea: **the first answer is raw material, not the result.** Most disappointment with AI follows the same pattern — someone gets a generic answer, decides “it just can't do this,” and writes it themselves instead. All it usually takes is one more sentence. But that sentence has to say **both direction and degree**: “make it better” doesn't work; “cut it in half, drop the examples, keep only the recommendation” always does.

## Today's prompt

Save this template to your notes. After twenty uses you'll stop needing it, because you'll start asking yourself these four questions automatically.

```
ROLE:
You are [profession or point of view] who [what defines
them — e.g. writes plainly and without buzzwords / looks for
risks / watches deadlines].

CONTEXT:
- Who I am and what I do: [one sentence]
- Situation: [what happened, where things stand now]
- Who the output is for and what they'll do with it: [recipient]
- Constraints: [time, scope, rules]
- What must not happen: [concern]
- Source material: [paste text, numbers, notes]

TASK:
[Verb] [exactly what] [scope].

FORMAT:
[length, structure, tone, language]

WHAT I DON'T WANT:
[buzzwords, generic advice, opening and closing summaries]
```

And when the answer comes back, don't walk away. Send one correction that states both direction and degree — for example: “Make this significantly more specific: back every general claim with a number, a date, or a name, or cut it. Shorter, 250 words max. Leave the paragraph about [topic] exactly as is. At the end, outside the text, list what you cut for length.”

That last line is the difference between trimming and losing something. Without it, the part you actually needed disappears too, and you won't notice until your reader does.
