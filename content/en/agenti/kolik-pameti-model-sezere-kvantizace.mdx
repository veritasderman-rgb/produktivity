You open a model library and under one name you see five files of different sizes. The same model, the same parameter count, but one is 4 GB and another 16. The difference is called quantisation, and it is the most useful thing you can know about local models.

## What it actually is

A model is a huge table of numbers — parameters. When its authors train it, each of those numbers is stored precisely, usually in 16 bits. Quantisation means you **round** those numbers and store them in fewer bits. Eight bits. Four. Sometimes fewer.

Just as when you write down a price of CZK 1,249.90 as “about twelve hundred”. You lose precision, you save space, and for most decisions it does not matter.

The values are approximate — they vary with the specific format and with which layers of the model are quantised how. The ratios hold, though.

## A formula you will remember

For a quick estimate in four-bit quantisation, which is the most common choice today:

**Billions of parameters × 0.6 ≈ gigabytes on disk and in memory.**

So:

| Model | Size at Q4 | Where it fits |
|---|---|---|
| 4 bn | ~2.5 GB | almost anything, even a laptop without a graphics card |
| 8 bn | ~5 GB | 8 GB graphics card, 16 GB Mac |
| 14 bn | ~9 GB | 12 GB graphics card, 24 GB Mac |
| 30 bn | ~18 GB | 24 GB graphics card, 32 GB Mac |
| 70 bn | ~42 GB | 64 GB Mac, multiple cards |

**And then add the context.** The conversation, the document you feed the model and its answer are all stored in memory on top. For a short chat that is hundreds of megabytes; for a long document, easily gigabytes. Hence the rule about headroom: **leave at least one to two gigabytes free above the size of the model.**

## Which variant to choose

In practice it is boringly simple:

- **Q4 is the default choice.** The best ratio of size to quality. When you download a model in Ollama without specifying anything, this is usually the variant you get.
- **Q5 or Q6 when you have memory to spare.** The difference from Q4 is small, but in precise tasks (code, numbers, structured output) it can show.
- **Q8 only exceptionally.** It takes twice as much as Q4, and the difference from Q5 is hard to measure in ordinary work.
- **Do not go below four bits.** Two- and three-bit variants exist, but the model starts to go noticeably wrong — mainly in precision and in sticking to instructions.

## A more important decision than you think

Here is the thing people underestimate: **a bigger model in heavy quantisation is almost always better than a smaller model at full precision.**

You have 12 GB of memory. You can choose:

- a model with 8 billion parameters at Q8 (~8.5 GB), or
- a model with 14 billion parameters at Q4 (~9 GB).

The second choice will be noticeably better in most tasks. The parameter count is a rough measure of how much the model “knows and can do”, and the loss from quantisation is trivial by comparison.

The rule: **first pick the largest model that fits in your memory at Q4, and only if there is room left, raise the precision.**

## What quantisation does not change

- **The speed of reading a long document.** That depends mainly on the length of the context and on the machine's performance.
- **The model's behaviour with instructions.** A Q4 model is not “dumber about rules”, just a little less precise in the details.
- **The quality of its Czech.** That is determined by training, not quantisation. Some model families are markedly better at Czech than others — which you can only find out by trying.

## How to try it in practice

Download two variants of the same model and ask them the same three questions:

```
ollama pull qwen3:8b
ollama pull qwen3:14b
```

Try the same trio on both: summarising a paragraph, pulling three pieces of data into a table and rephrasing a text. With the first two tasks you will often see no difference. With the third you usually will.

And then measure how long each model takes to answer:

```
ollama run qwen3:14b --verbose
```

The flag prints statistics after the answer, including generation speed. Below ten words per second, writing is unpleasant; above twenty it is fluent.

## What to watch out for

- **Do not download five variants “just in case”.** Each one takes up gigabytes. `ollama rm` is your friend.
- **Format labels differ by tool.** In Ollama you will see tags like `q4_K_M`, in LM Studio similar ones. The letters at the end indicate which layers are quantised differently — for making a decision, the number is enough.
- **Be sparing with context.** Giving a model a giant context window “just in case” takes up memory even if you never use it.
- **You recognise a model that does not fit by its speed, not by an error.** Nothing crashes. It just crawls. When an answer is suddenly ten times slower, you have run out of memory.
