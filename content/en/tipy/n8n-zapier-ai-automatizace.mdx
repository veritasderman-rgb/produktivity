Chatting with AI is manual labor: you carry data in, and you carry results out. An automation platform removes that back-and-forth — data reaches the AI on its own, and the result lands wherever you need it. You don't need to know how to code; you assemble steps like building blocks. And since we're talking about an automated system that touches your mail and your tasks, one rule applies from the start: **AI proposes, you approve.**

## A typical scenario

Freelancer Klára gets inquiries through a web form. She used to read each one by hand, copy it into a spreadsheet, create a “follow up” task, and write an initial reply. Ten inquiries a week meant two hours of copying — work that never actually required her to decide anything, just transcribe.

After building a single scenario, things look different. A new inquiry triggers the automation: AI pulls the name, project type, budget range, and deadline out of the text, writes a row into the spreadsheet, creates a task in her task manager labeled “needs approval,” and drafts a reply in her inbox. Klára goes through the drafts each morning, edits two, deletes one. She's still the one sending — she just no longer spends time on the copying.

## How to do it

1. **Pick one pain point, not five.** The best first scenario is something you do at least three times a week, is tedious, and has a clear start and end. Inquiries, invoices, meeting notes, new tasks from a form.
2. **Sketch three steps on paper.** What triggers it (the trigger), what should the AI do (the model step), where should the result go (the action). If it doesn't fit into three steps, the scenario is too big for a first attempt.
3. **Choose a platform.** Zapier is the easiest to get started with, Make offers clearer handling of branching logic, and n8n can be self-hosted on your own server. Decide based on where your data is allowed to go.
4. **Write the prompt like a brief for a temp worker.** What it receives as input, what it should return, in what format, and what to do if it's unsure. Add the line “If a piece of information isn't in the text, write ‘not specified’” — that limits guessing.
5. **Always make the output a draft.** A draft instead of a sent email, a task labeled “needs approval” instead of a task in a live project, a spreadsheet row to review. The automation must never have permission to send or delete.
6. **Test it against ten real cases.** Run the scenario manually over old data and compare what came out. Only then switch it on permanently.
7. **Have it notify you when something fails.** A scenario that breaks silently is worse than no scenario at all — you get used to relying on it.

## The best tools

- **Zapier** — the most widely used cloud platform with a huge number of integrations; the fastest path to a first working scenario without coding.
- **Make** — a visual scenario editor, clear even for more complex flows with conditions and branching.
- **n8n** — an open platform you can self-host on your own server; the choice for companies whose data can't go into someone else's cloud.
- **Built-in automations in tools you already have** — mail rules, Notion automations, Power Automate in Microsoft 365. When they're enough, there's no reason to add another service.
- **Agentic tools (Claude connected to your tools)** — instead of assembling steps, you describe the routine in sentences; useful when the requirements change often.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** one well-chosen scenario typically saves an hour or two a week. Three scenarios make automation the highest-return investment of your month.
- **Reliability:** an automation doesn't forget, doesn't put things off until tomorrow, and doesn't mistype a number while copying it.
- **Calm:** you stop having to remember the routine yourself. Your memory can be put to better use.
- **Control:** because everything ends up as a draft, your speed goes up, not your risk.

## Pro tip

Before you switch a scenario on, review what permissions you're giving it. An automation with access to your entire mailbox and the right to delete is way too much firepower for the task “copy an inquiry into a spreadsheet” — give it access to just one folder, and read-only at that. Send sensitive data (personal information, health data, trade secrets) exclusively to tools your company has approved; if you're unsure, either self-host with n8n or leave that data out of the automation entirely. And once a quarter, go through your list of scenarios and turn off the ones nobody uses anymore.
