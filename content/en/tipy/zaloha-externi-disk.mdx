Everyone puts off backing up, because it sounds like a project. Yet Windows and Mac both have backup built in and free — the only thing missing is a drive to back up to. Plug in an ordinary external drive, turn the feature on once, and the system keeps backing itself up from then on: every hour, quietly, including older versions of files. An overwritten thesis chapter or an accidentally deleted folder of invoices can then be restored in a couple of clicks. Cloud sync doesn't fully replace this — when you delete or overwrite a file, sync happily copies that mistake everywhere too.

## How to do it

1. Get an external drive with at least double the capacity of the data you want to protect, and connect it to your computer — ideally permanently, say into a dock or a port on the back of your monitor.
2. On Windows, open **Control Panel → System and Security → File History** and click **Turn on**. The system starts backing up files from your user folders (Documents, Pictures, Desktop…); the menu on the left lets you fine-tune what to skip and how long to keep versions.
3. On a Mac, go to **System Settings → General → Time Machine**, add the drive, and confirm. Time Machine keeps backing up automatically from then on and prunes old backups itself as space runs low.
4. Test a restore right away — a backup you've never tried restoring is just wishful thinking. On Windows: right-click a file or folder → **Properties → Previous Versions**. On a Mac, open Time Machine and browse versions through time.
5. Working on a laptop and don't want to carry the drive around? Leave it plugged into a dock at home — the backup catches up every time you connect. Just make that a rule, not an exception.

## A typical scenario

Translator Alena discovered on a Friday that her lengthy translation was empty after syncing — the file had gotten overwritten somewhere along the way, and the cloud had dutifully copied the empty version to every device. In the past that would have meant a week of work redone. This time she opened Previous Versions, picked Thursday afternoon, and had the document back within a minute. File History had been running quietly for months, unnoticed — exactly how a backup is supposed to work.

## What you get out of it

Protection against the three most common disasters: accidentally deleted, accidentally overwritten, drive died. And because the backup is automatic, it doesn't depend on your discipline — the only thing you have to watch is keeping the drive connected.

## Pro tip

Protect genuinely important data (invoices, contracts, family photos) twice over: an automatic backup to a drive plus the cloud. Two different mechanisms fail in different ways — which is exactly why they complement each other so well.
