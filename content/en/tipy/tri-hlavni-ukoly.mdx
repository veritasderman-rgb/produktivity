A long to-do list is a storeroom — a place where everything anyone ever thought of gets stashed, regardless of whether it's urgent, important, or just an interesting idea from a meeting three weeks ago. A plan for the day is something else: three items that have to be done before the day ends. Everything else is a bonus, not a failure if it doesn't happen.

## A typical scenario

You open your task manager in the morning and see twenty-seven items. Without picking three main tasks, the day turns into constantly hopping between small things — you answer a few emails, handle some admin, make a bit of progress on three different projects at once. By evening you're tired and yet can't say what actually happened today — everything and nothing at the same time.

With three chosen tasks, the day has a clear frame right from the morning: “if only these three things get done today, it was a good day.” The rest gets handled if there's time left, but the day's success isn't measured by the number of checked-off items — it's measured by whether these three are done.

## How to do it

1. In the morning (or better yet, the evening before, when you have more distance and calm), pick **3 tasks**, no more. The selection criterion: if only these three get done today, it was a good day, even if the rest of the list stays untouched.
2. Choose based on importance and impact, not on what's easiest to check off — a three-task list full of trivial items misses the point, which is to capture what actually matters.
3. Put the biggest or most demanding of the three first in your order, while you still have the most energy and focus — usually that's earlier in the day, not after lunch.
4. Handle the other two in whatever order makes sense given your scheduled meetings and energy, not mechanically by number.
5. In the evening, take two minutes for a quick check-in: what went well, what's carrying over to tomorrow and why it didn't happen — no self-criticism, just information for planning the next day.
6. If you don't finish everything, deliberately move the unfinished task to a specific day, not just back into the general list, where it could get lost among the other items.

## The best tools

- **Paper or a simple note** — your three tasks for the day don't need any app; a notebook on your desk or a note on your phone works just as well as anything more elaborate.
- **Todoist or another task manager** with the option to flag a task as priority or pin it to today — for anyone who wants to tie their three main tasks into the rest of their planning.
- **A calendar** — combining your three main tasks with timeboxing (assigning them specific time blocks) increases the odds they actually get done.
- **A journal or bullet journal** for physically writing them down and reflecting in the evening — a tactile record helps some people with the selection more than a digital list.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** an estimated hour a day, thanks to less jumping between tasks and clearer prioritization right from the morning.
- **A clear sense of a meaningful day:** even if you don't get to everything, three completed main tasks give you a concrete reason to call the day a success.
- **Less decision fatigue:** instead of picking from twenty-seven items over and over throughout the day, you've already decided what to focus on.
- **Better recognition of real priorities:** having to pick just three out of a long list forces you to think about what's actually important, not just what's urgent.

## Pro tip

If you keep struggling to get through your three tasks despite all your planning and effort, the problem isn't discipline — the tasks are simply too big. A daily task should be manageable in one to two hours of focused work, not spread across three days. If you're picking items that are actually whole projects, break them down into smaller steps before you put them among your daily three.
