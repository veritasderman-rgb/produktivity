The sneakiest hole in productivity isn't unfinished tasks — it's promises scattered across conversations. “I'll send it by Friday.” “I'll take a look.” “I'll get back to you next week.” Every one of those sentences is a commitment that nobody wrote down — and that the other side will remember in three days, not you. An evening routine plugs that hole: once a day it scans your mail and pulls out commitments before they turn into a problem.

## A typical scenario

Sales rep Marek wraps up a day with four calls and forty emails. At six he closes his laptop with the nagging feeling that “I still owed someone something,” but no idea what. On Friday a client calls, waiting on materials promised Tuesday. Marek apologizes and scrambles to find them — and in the process remembers he himself is still waiting on a quote from a supplier that was due a week ago.

With an evening routine, Marek gets a single summary at 5:30: three things he promised today, with deadlines; two things promised to him, including how long he's been waiting; and four emails older than two days with no reply. Draft tasks are already sitting in his task manager tagged “needs approval.” Marek runs through them in three minutes, deletes two, confirms three — and leaves work with a clear head.

## How to do it

1. **Decide what the routine should read.** At minimum, today's sent mail (that's where your promises live) and received mail (that's where everyone else's live). Optionally add meeting notes.
2. **Set up a scheduled run.** In an automation platform (Zapier, Make, n8n), build a scenario that fires every workday at a fixed time — typically half an hour before you wrap up.
3. **Write the prompt like a form.** Not “summarize my day,” but structured: “From these emails, pull out (a) what I promised whom, including the deadline, (b) what others promised me and since when I've been waiting, (c) emails with no reply older than two days. If no deadline was given, write ‘no deadline stated.’ Don't infer anything that isn't there.”
4. **Send the output somewhere you'll actually see it.** An email to yourself, a Slack message, a Notion page. A summary nobody opens is pointless.
5. **Create tasks automatically, but only as drafts.** Through your task manager's API (Todoist, Notion), have the routine create items tagged “needs approval” or drop them in a separate Inbox project. Never let them land directly among your confirmed tasks.
6. **Build in a thirty-second ritual.** You review the summary, confirm or delete the drafts. That's it — and it's the only point where a human enters the process.

## The best tools

- **Built-in AI in your inbox** (Gemini in Workspace, Copilot in Microsoft 365) — the simplest starting point if you just want a daily summary without creating tasks.
- **Zapier and Make** — no-code cloud platforms; they handle the scheduled trigger, the AI step, and writing into your task manager.
- **n8n** — a self-hosted option for companies that don't want to send inbox content to a third-party cloud service.
- **Todoist and Notion** — task managers with open APIs where drafts get created reliably and are easy to separate out with a tag.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** 15–25 minutes a day that would otherwise go into figuring out “what was I supposed to do” and writing apologetic emails.
- **Peace of mind:** no more evening rumination. Promises are written down, your head is clear — exactly the point of any decent work-organization method.
- **Outward reliability:** a broken promise damages trust more than a slow reply. The routine also tracks what you're waiting on.
- **Material for follow-ups:** a “who owes me what” list is ready-made material for a polite nudge.

## Pro tip

Let the routine work with silence too: the most valuable item in the summary is usually the list of emails nobody responded to. And hold the line — the automation may create, but never delete and never send. Security minimum: run the routine on a business account with contractual data protection, not a personal chat; exclude folders with sensitive matters from it, and explicitly forbid the prompt from reproducing whole email passages — one summary sentence and a link to the message is enough.
