Hunting for music is a classic time sink nobody talks about. You need fifteen seconds of a sting, or a quiet bed under a two-minute video — and you end up ninety minutes later, still clicking through stock libraries where everything is either too dramatic, too cheerful, or something everyone's already heard. Music generators flip the problem around: **instead of searching for a track that fits, you describe the track you want and get it back in a few minutes.** What's left to sort out isn't the technology — it's the license.

## A typical scenario

Marek, a video editor, is cutting a series of short how-to videos for a client. Every episode needs a five-second sting and an unobtrusive background bed. Until now, each episode cost him roughly ninety minutes of searching through a library he pays for — and he still ended up reaching for the same track a third time, because he never found anything better.

With a generator, the process looks different: he writes “calm instrumental bed, gentle piano and muted strings, no drums, 120 seconds, neutral mood, must not compete with the voiceover,” generates six variants, plays two against the finished video, and uses one. Twenty minutes including listening. But before he sends the finished episode to the client, he does one more thing most people skip: he checks exactly what his tier of that service actually allows him to do with the music.

## How to do it

1. **Write the brief like a director, not a fan.** State the genre, instruments, tempo, mood, length, and above all the function — “under a voiceover,” “opening sting,” “tension before a reveal.” The more specific the description, the fewer rounds it takes.
2. **Say what shouldn't be there too.** “No vocals,” “no drums,” “no strong melody” move the result further than three extra adjectives would. For a background bed, the most common mistake is a track that's too interesting on its own.
3. **Generate several variants and listen to them in context.** A track that sounds great on its own can get in the way under a voiceover. Play the candidates directly against your video or your own voice.
4. **Have it regenerate a longer version or a different ending.** Most services can extend a track or rework the ending, so you avoid a jarring cut in the middle of a phrase.
5. **Sort out the license before you deploy it anywhere.** For internal use — presentations, prototypes, a concept for a client, schoolwork — this is simple and worry-free. For anything commercial or publicly published, open the service's current terms and actually read them.
6. **Save the prompt, not just the track.** If you need a second episode with the same sound six months from now, the prompt saves you the entire first step. It belongs in your [prompt library](/tipy/ai-knihovna-promptu) just like your text prompts do.

## The best tools

- **[Suno](https://suno.com)** — the most widely used generator; handles both instrumentals and vocal tracks, and offers track extension.
- **[Udio](https://udio.com)** — similar range, a different character to the output; worth trying both on the same brief and comparing.
- **Stock libraries (Artlist, Epidemic Sound)** — still the safest choice wherever you need an absolutely clean license for commercial use.
- **Built-in libraries in editing software** — often plenty for quick internal material, with the license already covered by your subscription.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** from hours of searching down to tens of minutes. For serialized content, this is the biggest saving of all.
- **Fit:** the music matches the length and the mood, because it was made to measure instead of you adapting to it.
- **Distinctiveness:** your sting won't sound like the sting on three other podcasts using the same stock bundle.
- **Room to experiment:** when a variant costs two minutes, you can throw out five of them and it doesn't matter.

## Pro tip

The licensing reality is more sobering than the ads let on. For commercial use you need the matching paid tier at these services — on free accounts, output is typically restricted to personal, non-commercial use. Terms differ between services and change over time, and the copyright status of AI-generated music is still unsettled: in many countries, work without human authorship is protected only in a limited way, so it may not belong to you the way you'd expect. **Before any commercial deployment, open the service's current license terms and save a dated copy of them** — on a client job, that's the difference between “covered” and “hoping for the best.” For internal work, prototypes, and schoolwork, you don't need to worry about any of this. And if it's a major campaign or a long-term brand, consider a traditional license instead — music is the cheapest line item in the budget to risk cutting corners on.
