Text-to-speech had one long-standing flaw: it sounded like a robot. That's changed over the last couple of years. New models read naturally, hold intonation, and sound convincing enough that a listener stops noticing it isn't a person after about thirty seconds. And that has a practical consequence: **content you've already written, you can now offer as audio for free or nearly free** — and reach people who never have time to read but do have an hour in the car.

## A typical scenario

Tomáš, a consultant, writes a newsletter twice a month for two hundred clients. Open rates are decent, but click-throughs on longer pieces are miserable — people “save them for later” and never come back to them. He tried recording one himself once: twenty minutes of recording, forty minutes of cutting out stumbles, a result he'd only ever use once. He didn't try again.

Now his process looks different: he pastes the finished text into a voice generator, has an MP3 in two minutes, and attaches it to the email and embeds it as a player on the site. It costs him less than an hour a month. After three months he notices roughly one in five readers reaches for the audio version — and they tend to be his busiest readers, the clients he cares about most. Eliška, a student, uses the same trick for her course notes: she converts them to audio and listens while running.

## How to do it

1. **Pick a voice and spend a little time with it.** Services offer dozens of voices; quality varies more than you'd expect depending on the language and accent. Try the same paragraph across five voices and pick the one that sounds like you or like your content. You only make this choice once.
2. **Prepare the text for the ear, not the eye.** Remove bullet points nobody can follow without seeing them, spell out abbreviations, numbers, and units. A short extra intro helps too: “This is the audio version of an article about…” The edit takes a few minutes and it shows in the result.
3. **Generate it and listen to the whole thing.** Really, the whole thing. Ninety percent of the output is usually fine, but one badly read name or number can ruin the impression. Problem words can simply be rewritten phonetically in the input.
4. **Put the audio wherever the content already lives.** As a player alongside the article, as a link to the file in the newsletter (most email clients won't play audio inline), as an attachment on company storage for an internal memo.
5. **If you do this regularly, automate it.** These services have APIs, so you can build a chain: new article → text goes to the generator → the MP3 gets saved alongside the article. If that feels out of reach, the steps are in [the chapter on AI and automation](/prirucka/ai-a-automatizace) — this is exactly the level where a chat habit turns into a routine.
6. **Check whether anyone's actually listening.** If it's zero after three months, save yourself the work. If it's not, add audio versions of your older articles too.

## The best tools

- **[ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io)** — the most natural results across many languages, with a simple web interface and an API for automation.
- **Google Cloud Text-to-Speech and Azure Speech** — reliable enterprise options with clear contractual terms; a good fit when the audio comes from internal documents.
- **NotebookLM** — turns your source material straight into a two-voice spoken discussion; a different genre from straight narration, but very strong for study material.
- **Your phone's built-in read-aloud feature** — try it before generating anything at all; for personal use it's often enough, and it costs nothing.

## What you get out of it

- **Reach:** the same content now reaches people in the car, on a walk, or doing the dishes. For a lot of readers, that's the only time they can actually take you in.
- **Time:** minutes instead of an hour of recording and editing. An audio version stops being a project and becomes a routine part of publishing.
- **Accessibility:** people with visual impairments or dyslexia can get to your content without workarounds.
- **Quality:** listening to your own text catches awkward sentences better than any proofread. Whatever sounds wrong was usually written wrong.

## Pro tip

Most services can clone a voice from a few minutes of recording. It's tempting, and it's legitimate for your own voice — your brand, your sound, your consent. But hold a hard line: **only clone your own voice, or the voice of someone who has given clearly documented consent.** Imitating a colleague's, a client's, or a public figure's voice is a legal and ethical problem, not a joke — a voice is personal data, and misusing it is now a standard tool for fraud. And more broadly: send texts with sensitive information (HR, health, financial) to a generator only on a paid or business account with contractual data protection, never the free tier, and anonymize whatever can be anonymized beforehand.
