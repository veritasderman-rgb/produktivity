Reading an email and leaving it in your inbox “for later” means reading it twice — once now, and again once you come back to it and have to reconstruct the context from scratch. The two-minute rule breaks that cycle with a simple test: if a task takes less than two minutes, you do it right away. If it takes longer, it goes to your task list with a deadline, and the email disappears from your inbox. The result is an inbox you read once, for good.

## A typical scenario

Jana runs a small team and opens her inbox in the morning to find forty unread messages. Without the rule, the scenario is always the same: she clicks through the first three emails, doesn't have a ready answer for the fourth, leaves it “for later,” and opens the fifth. An hour later, she's read twenty messages, replied to three, and is left with a vague sense that something's hanging over her — but she'll have to dig around again to remember exactly what.

With the rule, it looks different. Jana goes through the same forty emails, but decides on each one immediately: a short reply gets written on the spot, a bigger task gets moved to her task list with a deadline, a purely informational message just gets read and archived. After the same hour, her inbox is empty and her task list has three new items with clear deadlines — instead of twenty unresolved threads.

## How to do it

1. Only open an email when you actually have time to do something about it — skimming “just quickly” between meetings is exactly the habit this rule is meant to fix.
2. The moment you open an email, decide right away how much time it needs. A short reply, a forward, or a confirmation under two minutes → handle it **right now** and archive the email.
3. Anything over two minutes (you need supporting material, need to think through a response, need to ask a coworker) → create a task in your task list with a deadline and archive the email immediately — it doesn't stay in your inbox as a reminder.
4. Purely informational messages (CCs, newsletters, order confirmations) → read and archive, no further step needed.
5. The goal is an inbox that ends up empty or nearly empty after you go through it — not because you deleted work, but because every message already has its place: either it's done, or it's waiting on your task list with a deadline.

## The best tools

- **One-key archiving (Gmail, Outlook)** — both apps have a built-in shortcut for archiving without deleting; it's worth finding it in your shortcut settings and using it automatically.
- **Quick Steps in Outlook / canned responses in Gmail** — for recurring short replies (confirmations, forwards), these save writing from scratch every time.
- **Todoist, Microsoft To Do, or another task manager** — this is where everything over two minutes belongs; integration with email (adding a task with one click from an email) speeds the rule up significantly.
- **Snooze** — if an email needs handling but not today, snoozing it is better than leaving it sitting in your inbox as visual clutter.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** roughly 15–20 minutes a day that would otherwise go to rereading the same messages and figuring out what you actually meant to do with them.
- **Peace of mind:** an empty inbox isn't about aesthetics — it's confirmation that nothing is left hanging, because every message has a decision attached to it.
- **Fewer forgotten tasks:** anything that requires time gets a deadline on your task list, instead of just the hope that you'll remember it next time you're scrolling through your inbox.

## Pro tip

Your inbox is not a task list: a task like “reply to Smith about the proposal” belongs in your task system, where it has a deadline and a priority, not in your email, where it gets lost among dozens of other messages. When “to handle” messages start piling up in your inbox, that's a signal you're not applying the rule in that moment — go back to step 2 and decide on each one again.
