Most of your mail doesn't need a decision — it just needs to be filed. Notifications, CCs, newsletters, order confirmations. Yet you scroll through it anyway, because it sits in the same pile as the three emails that genuinely need your head. The goal of triage isn't zero inbox — it's **an inbox that contains only work for a human**. Everything else should be visible whenever you choose to look.

## A typical scenario

Lenka, an accountant, gets around ninety emails a day. Realistically, eight of them are actually hers to deal with. The rest are automated messages from the billing system, CCs from projects where she's only there for information, and mailing lists. She still skims every single one, afraid something important might be hiding among them. That's half an hour a day spent scrolling through things that require no decision at all.

After setting up triage, her inbox looks different: the main folder holds nine messages, all from people, all waiting for a reply. Automated messages go to a System folder, informational CCs to a To Read folder, mailing lists to Newsletters. Lenka checks them once a day after lunch — or skips it on a packed day, and nothing breaks.

## How to do it

1. **Hard rules first, AI second.** A plain filter by sender or subject line catches most of the clutter. Set up rules for automated messages from the systems you use and for mailing lists. A rule is free, instant, and never gets it wrong.
2. **Define categories, not hundreds of labels.** Four tend to work well: needs a reply, FYI only, system messages, mailing lists. More categories means more decisions — the exact opposite of the point.
3. **Turn on built-in sorting.** Both Gmail and Outlook offer automatic categories and separation of lower-priority mail; in Outlook that's the Focused Inbox feature. Let it run for a week and watch what it misses.
4. **Add AI for what a rule can't handle.** A rule can't tell "request for a quote" apart from "notice about a changed deadline." An automation scenario (Zapier, Make, n8n) sends the message text to AI with instructions to place it into one of your categories and return only the category name — and the label gets applied based on the result.
5. **Never let it delete automatically.** Triage may move and label, not remove. A misfiled message sitting in a folder can be found; a deleted one can't.
6. **Check it after a week.** Go through what ended up outside the main inbox and adjust the rules. After two rounds of tuning, accuracy is usually high enough to trust the folders.

## The best tools

- **Built-in filters and labels in Gmail, rules and Focused Inbox in Outlook** — the cheapest and fastest step, and your data never leaves your account. Start here.
- **Gemini in Workspace and Copilot in Microsoft 365** — help with sorting and summarizing right inside an environment your company has already approved.
- **SaneBox** — a service specialized in separating low-priority mail into its own folders; it works on top of your existing inbox without changing your client.
- **Zapier, Make, or n8n** — for custom categories and custom logic; pick n8n if the content of your mail shouldn't leave your own infrastructure.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** 20–40 minutes a day depending on your mail volume. It's not just about reading — it's about dozens of micro-decisions of "is this for me?"
- **Peace of mind:** the inbox stops being an endless list. Nine items are manageable; ninety aren't.
- **Fewer things missed:** counterintuitively, the risk of overlooking something important actually drops — important messages aren't buried in the noise anymore.
- **Faster response time:** when only things that need handling sit in the inbox, they get handled faster.

## Pro tip

Name one folder "Review sorting" and let messages the AI wasn't sure about land there — allow it to answer "I don't know" in the prompt. Uncertainty routed to its own folder beats uncertainty hidden under the wrong label. And watch security: triage sends the content of your emails to a model, so run it through a company account with contractual data protection. Exclude folders with sensitive matters from automation entirely, and instruct the model to return only a category name, never a retelling of the content.
