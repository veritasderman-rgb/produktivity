There are hundreds of Pomodoro apps, but how many of them do you actually keep using for more than a week? A physical timer works better — for three reasons a digital app simply can't match, because it lives on the very same device as everything that distracts you. The method itself is simple: you work in focused 25-minute blocks with short breaks in between, but the form your timer takes decides whether you actually stick with it.

## A typical scenario

Developer Klára tried Pomodoro apps maybe ten times — it always worked for a few days, and then the timer became just another icon on her phone, one she'd tap when distracted and immediately see a social media notification right next to it. Ten seconds later she was in a different app instead of working, and had forgotten about the timer entirely.

Since she got a mechanical timer for her desk, the difference has been noticeable. She winds it, sets it next to her monitor, and hears the ticking and sees the time run down in her peripheral vision — without ever touching her phone.

## How to do it

1. Get a **mechanical kitchen timer** (the most accessible option, available at any home goods store), a **TimeFlip** (a cube you turn to the side showing the interval you want, and it starts timing on its own), or a **Timeular** (a similar principle, with the added benefit of linking to time tracking for individual projects).
2. Wind the timer to 25 minutes, set it somewhere visible next to your monitor, and work until it rings.
3. When it rings, take a short break, usually around 5 minutes, then repeat the cycle.
4. After four completed rounds, treat yourself to a longer break, fifteen to twenty minutes, before starting the next set.
5. In an open-plan office with coworkers, consider a quieter option — vibration or a visual signal instead of a bell, so the timer itself doesn't disturb your focus.
6. Position the timer so it's visible peripherally, not directly in your line of sight — the goal is to sense it out of the corner of your eye, not to stare at it instead of your work.
7. If you need to break your focus before the timer rings (an urgent call, a coworker with a question), simply stop the timer and start a new round from the beginning when you return — a half-completed interval doesn't count.

## The best tools

- **A mechanical kitchen timer** — the most accessible and simplest option, works without any apps, available anywhere.
- **TimeFlip** — a cube that measures time automatically based on which side it's resting on; handy when you're tracking several kinds of activity at once.
- **Timeular** — a similar principle to TimeFlip, with the added benefit of linking to software that tracks time spent on individual projects or clients.
- **A 25-minute hourglass** — the simplest visual option with no buttons at all, good for people who want to literally see time running out.
- **An offline digital kitchen timer** — a middle ground between a mechanical timer and an app: precise countdown, but no notifications or extra distractions.

## What you get out of it

- **A visible reminder:** you see the time running out in your peripheral vision, while an app on your phone or computer is hidden behind other windows and easy to forget about.
- **No detours to your phone:** setting a timer on your phone has one fatal flaw — you pick up the phone, and "I'll just set the timer" turns into five minutes of scrolling through notifications. That adds up to roughly 10 extra minutes a day.
- **A tactile focus ritual:** winding the timer is a physical, tangible "now I'm working" signal. Your brain gradually links it to a state of focus, much like it links other rituals to sleep or exercise.
- **Simplicity:** no app setup, no notifications, no choosing between dozens of similar Pomodoro apps.
- **A portable habit:** a physical object on your desk works the same at home, in the office, or in a café, regardless of which device you're using.

## Pro tip

Always put the timer in the same spot — after a few days, your brain will associate its position with work mode, the same way it associates a desk with work. Moving it somewhere else (say, to the kitchen for lunch) breaks that signal — which is exactly what you want during a break.
