There's one thing no song on the radio or YouTube can do: sing your child's name. The moment a four-year-old first hears "Charlie, Charlie, today is your day" and realizes the song is actually about him — that can't really be described, only experienced. And then comes phase two, which every parent who's tried this knows: the child wants to hear it again. And again. And tomorrow, too. And over the phone to grandma.

**This is exactly the kind of AI use case where five minutes of work produces something that's worth years at home.** No productivity angle, no hours saved — just a joy you can't buy any other way, because no label has ever released, or will ever release, a song about Ellie, who learned to swim this year and loves horses. Music generators put it together in a few minutes, complete with music, vocals, and a chorus the family is still humming in September.

This guide walks through the whole process: everything you can make, the two paths for making it (more reliable with lyrics from Claude, faster straight in the generator), what the free tier can handle, how rights and your child's name fit in — and, at the end, an idea that can turn into a family tradition. One rule sits above all of it, the same one that applies to [bedtime stories on demand](/tipy/ai-v-rodine-vychova-hry-pohadky): **the machine makes it, the parent listens to the whole thing, and only then does it reach the kids.**

## What you can actually make

Before your first attempt, walk through the menu. Most parents think of a birthday song — and stop there, even though the everyday ones tend to be the most valuable.

- **Family anthem.** A song about your family: names, the dog, Sunday pancakes, the keys that are always missing. Plays in the car at the start of a trip, and at Christmas. Works for every age — and hits the adults hardest.
- **Birthday song with name and age.** "Ellie just turned eight" in the chorus, this year's wins in the verses. Plays at the cake, then all year long. From age two up — there's no upper limit.
- **Custom lullaby.** Slow, rocking, with your child's nickname and their stuffed animal in the lyrics. For the youngest, up to about age five; the bonus is that it never gets old for you, because it's yours.
- **Routine songs.** Picking up toys, getting dressed, and above all brushing teeth — a tooth-brushing song is motivational gold: it plays for exactly as long as brushing takes, and turns an annoying chore into a ritual. Works best between roughly ages three and seven.
- **Road-trip song.** Your own "we're going on a trip" song with the places you actually go and your family's own habits. Keeps everyone occupied, brings everyone together, and after three trips everyone knows it by heart.
- **Birthday song for a party.** Doesn't have to be for your own child — a song for a friend who's celebrating is a gift no one else will bring. Make the chorus a singalong so every kid at the party joins in.
- **Learning song.** The alphabet, times tables, days of the week, months. Whatever's in the chorus gets remembered — the principle behind half of early-childhood education, now custom-built for whatever your child is currently struggling with. From preschool through middle school.
- **Team anthem.** Youth soccer, a scout troop, a summer-camp cabin team. Names in the verses, a chant in the chorus — a team with its own anthem holds together differently.

The same thing is true of every one of these: the power isn't in the musical quality, it's in the fact that it's **about them**. A kid forgives all kinds of things; their own name in the chorus, never — in the best possible way.

## Path A: lyrics from Claude, music from Suno

The more reliable of the two paths. The division of labor makes sense: **Claude is good at lyrics and understands a brief like "for a four-year-old, simple words, name in the chorus." Suno is good at music and singing.** Leave the lyrics to the music generator and you'll get a generic song where the name might show up, and might come out mangled; have Claude write it and you check it, and you're holding everything that matters before the first note even plays.

The process has five steps: a prompt for Claude, a parent check of the lyrics (out loud!), pasting them into Suno's Custom mode, generating variations, and picking one and listening to the whole track. We'll go through all five with ready-to-use prompts — just fill in the brackets.

### How Suno works, in a nutshell

Suno is the most widely used music generator: you describe what you want, and it hands back a finished track with vocals. For our path, the key thing is **Custom mode**: it has two fields. The **Lyrics** field holds the words of the song, broken up by tags in square brackets — [Verse 1], [Chorus], [Bridge] — that tell Suno what's a verse and what's a chorus. The **Style** field holds a short description of the music: genre, mood, instruments, type of voice. Keep the style description short and concrete.

Every generation returns two variations of the same song — always listen to both, they differ more than you'd expect. And keep in mind that the same prompt won't return the same track twice: when one turns out well, save it right away.

### Step 1: the prompt for Claude — family anthem

```text
Write the lyrics for a family anthem that I'll turn into a song with an
AI music generator.

About us: we're a [family of four] — [mom, dad, Ellie (age 7)
and Charlie (age 4), our dog Buddy].
What captures us: [Sunday pancakes, weekend trips to the cabin, dad
always losing his keys, bedtime reading].

Rules:
- 2 verses of 4 short lines each, and a 4-line chorus
- the chorus should be simple enough for a four-year-old to sing —
  max 6 words per line, no clusters of hard consonants
- every family member's name should appear at least once, in a
  form that sings well
- specific images from our life (pancakes, keys, the dog), no
  generic lines about love and family togetherness
- mark up the lyrics with [Verse 1], [Chorus], [Verse 2] in
  square brackets — the generator understands these tags

At the end, add two sentences: which spots might be hard to sing
cleanly, and what simpler words could go there instead.
```

This returns lyrics ready to paste into Suno, tags included. Check two things: that no line reads like a greeting card ("family is the greatest gift") — cut it and ask for a more specific image instead — and that names appear in the form your family actually uses. The model likes to "fix" Charlie into Charles.

### Step 1, take two: birthday songs and party songs

```text
Write the lyrics for a birthday song. I'm turning it into music with
an AI generator and it'll play by the cake.

Birthday kid: [Ellie], turning [8].
What she's mastered this year and what she loves: [learned to swim,
reads on her own, loves horses and the color purple].
Who's coming to the party: [six kids, ages 6-9].

Rules:
- verse + chorus + verse + chorus, 120 words total, max
- the chorus needs to include her name and age — and be a
  singalong: short, repeatable, so even kids meeting Ellie for
  the first time can join in
- this year's wins go in the verses, but keep it light — it's a
  party, not a report card
- no jokes at the kid's expense, nothing that could embarrass her
  in front of friends
- simple rhythm, no tongue-twisters
- tags: [Verse 1], [Chorus], [Verse 2], [Chorus]

At the end, add a second version of the chorus in case the first
doesn't sit right in the rhythm.
```

The same prompt works for a friend's birthday with a small tweak ("I'm not the birthday kid's parent, I want to make this song as a gift — here's what I know about them…"). One thing to watch: get the other parents' okay first that a song with their child's name will exist, and where it'll be played. It's their call to make, the same way it would be yours.

### Step 1, take three: the lullaby

```text
Write the lyrics for a lullaby for a [three-year-old]. We call him
[Mouse] — use that nickname.

Conditions:
- 2 short verses and a chorus, 80 words total, max
- slow, rocking rhythm: long vowels, soft words, no harsh
  consonant clusters
- images: [the moon outside the window, the blanket, a stuffed
  rabbit] — nothing scary, nobody leaves, nothing gets lost
- the chorus should be hummable even without words, in case the
  parent runs out of them
- the last line ends in falling asleep, not a punchline
- no words a three-year-old wouldn't know
- tags: [Verse 1], [Chorus], [Verse 2]
```

For the lullaby, the parent check matters more than anywhere else: read the lyrics through the eyes of a child who'll be alone in the dark in five minutes. What sounds poetic to an adult ("the night will carry you off to dreamland") can sound frightening to a three-year-old. Nobody who's ever put a kid to bed is shy about cutting a line.

### Step 1, take four: routine songs

```text
Write the lyrics for a motivational tooth-brushing song for a
[five-year-old]. It'll play in the bathroom and needs to last
exactly as long as brushing does.

Conditions:
- length for about 2 minutes of singing: 2 verses, chorus twice
- the chorus is a simple call tied to a motion (the toothbrush
  dances, up top, down low) — memorable after one listen
- the verses should walk through the steps: top, bottom, front,
  back, tongue — the song guides the brushing, not just entertains
  during it
- upbeat tempo, no scaring the kid with the dentist, cavities, or
  germs
- tags: [Verse 1], [Chorus], [Verse 2], [Chorus]

Add a calmer version of the chorus for evening brushing.
```

The same template works for cleaning up toys (steps: blocks into the bin, books onto the shelf…) and getting dressed (socks, pants, sweater — in the order they're actually put on). The trick is always the same: **the steps of the routine belong in the lyrics**, so the song walks the child through the task and the parent doesn't have to give orders.

### Step 2: check the lyrics — out loud

Before you send the lyrics to Suno, read them **out loud and in rhythm**. Not with your eyes — out loud. A line you stumble over will trip up the generator too. If you're not sure, run the lyrics through one more check:

```text
Here are the lyrics for a children's song I'm going to have an AI
generator sing:

[paste the lyrics]

Check for:
1. Words the generator will likely stumble over — consonant
   clusters, long compound words. For each one, suggest a
   simpler replacement with the same meaning.
2. Lines that are a syllable longer or shorter than the rest —
   fix them.
3. The name [Ellie]: list every form it appears in, and flag
   whether any of them could be tricky to sing.
4. Is the chorus simple enough for a [five-year-old] to sing
   after two listens?

Don't rewrite the whole thing — just flag the spots and offer
alternatives.
```

This returns a list of risky spots. The ban on a full rewrite is deliberate — otherwise the model would "improve" the lyrics into something more generic, and you'd lose the family details the whole song exists for.

### Step 3: pasting into Suno and style tags

In Suno, switch on Custom mode, paste the lyrics with their tags into the Lyrics field, and write a description of the music into the Style field. Keep it concrete — genre, mood, two or three instruments, type of voice. Four tested starting points for our four genres:

```text
Family anthem:
upbeat folk pop, acoustic guitar, accordion, happy singalong,
male and female vocals, catchy simple chorus

Birthday and party song:
cheerful children's pop, playful, handclaps, glockenspiel,
bright female vocals, party mood, singalong chorus

Lullaby:
gentle lullaby, soft female vocals, music box, slow tempo,
warm, calm, minimal instruments

Routine song (teeth, cleanup):
energetic children's song, bouncy rhythm, ukulele, playful
vocals, fun, simple and repetitive
```

The child's name and the song's content don't belong in the style field — that's what the lyrics are for. And don't put band or artist names in there ("like [a specific artist]"): services block that, and it wouldn't improve the result anyway. Describe the sound, not the reference.

### Step 4: variations and pronunciation

Generate both variations and give them a listen. English is Suno's home turf, so pronunciation is reliably solid — but "reliably" isn't "always." Once in a while the stress lands on the wrong syllable, a long word slurs together, or a name comes out sounding odd. What to do about it — in this order:

1. **Regenerate.** Same prompt, new attempt. This alone often fixes it — every generation sings a little differently, and the third pass can nail exactly what the first one fumbled.
2. **Simplify the word.** Swap the clunky spot in the lyrics for something shorter. "Extraordinary" almost never comes out clean; "amazing" almost always does.
3. **Try a different form of the name.** If the generator mangles Charlie, try Charles, Chuck, or a nickname your family actually uses. Most names have more than one shape, and one of them usually falls into the rhythm on its own.
4. **Move the important stuff into the chorus.** The chorus repeats, so even if it comes out a little rough the first time, your brain fills in the gaps by the second and third pass. A verse doesn't get that second chance.
5. **Multilingual families and unusual names, take extra passes.** If your household mixes languages, or your child's name comes from a language Suno handles less confidently, expect more of these hiccups than average — lean harder on steps 1 through 4, and budget an extra generation or two. For a lullaby especially, it's worth the extra pass: that's the one place where the words themselves carry the moment.

Change one thing at a time, or you won't know what actually helped — the same principle as [iterating with AI](/tipy/ai-iterace). And don't chase perfection: a song for your kid isn't a radio master. A slightly rough consonant in the chorus won't be remembered a month from now; the name and the joy will.

### Step 5: listen to the whole track

This last step doesn't get skipped: **listen to the whole track from start to finish before you play it for the kids.** The generator occasionally sings something other than what's in the lyrics, adds a passage you didn't write, or ends the track strangely. For a song your child is going to hear fifty times, you want to know exactly what's in it — all three and a half minutes.

### Bonus: the learning song

The most practical genre, saved for last. Whatever's in the chorus is what a child remembers — so use that on purpose:

```text
Write the lyrics for an educational song for a [seven-year-old]
about [the five times table].

Rules:
- the material being taught belongs in the chorus, because the
  chorus is what repeats and sticks: [the sequence 5, 10, 15, 20,
  25...] built into the rhythm
- the verses explain it through images ([five fingers on a hand,
  a nickel]), not through a rule
- nursery-rhyme rhythm, steady — it should be chantable even
  without music
- 100 words max, tags [Verse 1], [Chorus], [Verse 2], [Chorus]

At the end, list every number and fact that appears in the lyrics
so I can double-check them — a rhyme can easily nudge a number out
of place, and a song that teaches a mistake is worse than no song.
```

The last paragraph of the prompt matters: for a learning song, **check the facts by hand.** The model can slip "five times six is twenty-five" into a rhyme because it scans better than the truth — and a song that teaches a mistake is worse than no song at all. For the alphabet, check the letter order; for days of the week, check the sequence.

## Path B: everything inside Suno

The faster path: type a simple description into Suno and let it come up with both lyrics and music at once. It looks something like this:

```text
An upbeat children's song about a family driving on a road trip.
Simple catchy chorus, acoustic guitar, playful vocals, under
three minutes.
```

When this is enough: when **the exact wording doesn't matter.** An instrumental lullaby for falling asleep (type "instrumental," you don't need vocals), background music for playtime, a quick test of whether the kids like a style, a song for the car when the words themselves aren't the point. It's also the fastest way to get a feel for Suno the first time — your first track is done in two minutes, and that experience is worth having.

When Path A pays off: as soon as the song is supposed to be **about someone specific.** In this mode the generator writes its own lyrics, and the result tends to be generic — the name might show up once instead of in the chorus, the age gets dropped, and family details get replaced by generic rhymes about sunshine. And there's a bigger issue: you only see the lyrics together with the finished music, so every fix means regenerating the whole track. On top of that, lyrics written by Claude and checked by a parent are almost always more specific and natural than lyrics a music generator comes up with on its own.

Practical rule: **Path B for testing things out and instrumentals, Path A for anything with a name in it.**

## Alternatives

Suno isn't the only option — and it's worth knowing what pays off when.

**ElevenLabs and Eleven Music.** ElevenLabs, known mainly for speech synthesis, also has a music generator called Eleven Music: it builds a full track, vocals included, from a text prompt, sings in vocals across dozens of languages, and lets you edit a finished track section by section. It's mainly interesting for parents who already use ElevenLabs for [turning text into audio](/tipy/elevenlabs-audioverze-obsahu) — you get music and spoken word under one roof. You can try it in a limited way without paying; broader use and commercial rights are tied to paid tiers, so check the exact terms with the service. Worth noting: ElevenLabs licenses its music through agreements with music publishers, which is an argument that matters more for businesses sleeping easy at night — for a song in a kid's bedroom, it makes no difference.

**Udio.** The other well-known generator has gone through a major shift: after settling a dispute with music publishers, it's rebuilding itself into a licensed platform where tracks live inside the service and come out only in limited ways. For a parent who mainly wants a song file to put on their phone and play in the car, that makes it impractical right now — but the situation is evolving, so if it appeals to you, check the current download terms.

**What about the spoken word?** Bedtime stories made to order are a different discipline with their own rules — you'll find the full process, prompts included, in the guide to [AI in family life](/tipy/ai-v-rodine-vychova-hry-pohadky). A song and a story complement each other nicely: the story for bedtime, the lullaby for falling asleep.

For ordinary family use, Suno is the simplest place to start: the biggest community, the most guides, and a free tier that's plenty for playing around at home.

## Free, or paid?

The honest answer: **the free tier of Suno covers everything in this article.** At the time of writing, it gives you a daily credit allowance good for roughly ten songs a day — and since every generation returns two variations, that's really five attempts. For one birthday song you'll regenerate two or three times, that's plenty; for a family anthem, you might end up waiting until the next day. Credits refresh daily, so patience substitutes for money.

Where the free tier hits a wall:

- **Commercial use.** Tracks from the free tier are for personal, non-commercial use only. That's no problem at all for a song at home, in the car, or at a party — you'd only cross the line by monetizing it (an ad-supported video, selling it, a paid event).
- **Queue and speed.** Free generation runs in a shared queue, so you'll wait a bit during peak times. For an impatient kid at the screen, that's an argument for generating ahead of time, not for paying.
- **Volume and extra features.** Anyone generating daily — say, if birthday songs become your specialty for the whole extended family — will hit the daily limit. Paid tiers add substantially more credits, a newer model version, priority queueing, and advanced editing, plus, importantly, commercial rights to tracks made while you're subscribed.

An important detail if commercial use ever matters to you: **what counts is the tier at the moment the track was created, not when it's published.** A song generated for free doesn't become commercially usable just because you subscribe to something later. For a detailed breakdown of the licensing reality of generated music — for parents running a podcast or channel — see the guide to [AI music: intros and background tracks](/tipy/ai-hudba-na-pozadi-projektu).

The takeaway for parents: start free. Paying makes sense once you know you're using this regularly — or once you want to play the songs publicly somewhere you're making money from it.

## Rights and privacy

A short chapter, but it belongs here — four things that are fair to know.

**At home, you're fine.** A song generated for your own kids, played at home, in the car, and at a party, is non-commercial personal use. Don't go looking for a problem here.

**Your child's name doesn't belong on public social media.** A song with your child's name, age, and details from their life is adorable — and that's exactly why it's tempting to show it off publicly. Don't. It's a publicly searchable trace of your child that you're the one creating, and it can't be taken back; the same principle as photos, except with a name in the chorus, the searchability is literal. If you want to show it off, send the song straight to grandma, or generate a nameless version for public sharing. And remember that on the free tier, publishing anything tied to earnings is outside the license anyway.

**Only a first name in the prompt, nothing more.** The song needs a name — that's the whole point of it — but it doesn't need a last name, a school, an address, or a birth date. "Ellie, age 8, loves horses" is plenty. The same hygiene applies here as with anything else you write about your kids into a chat.

**Melodies of well-known songs don't work — and it's worth knowing why.** The tempting shortcut — "write it to the tune of Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star" — doesn't work, for two reasons. Technically: you're describing a style to the generator, not notes — it doesn't take a specific melody as input and won't reliably reproduce one, even one it "knows." And legally: for modern songs, the melody is a protected work, and services block requests like "in the style of [a specific artist]." For folk songs and nursery rhymes the copyright expired long ago, but the technical obstacle remains — you'll get something that only distantly resembles Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star, and a kid who knows the original will notice immediately. Let a new melody happen: kids don't compare it to the original, they remember the one that's theirs.

And the closing rule of this whole chapter, underlined twice: **the parent listens to the entire result before playing it for the kids.** From the first second to the last. It's a three-minute safeguard, and the only one that exists in the chain between machine and child.

## Common mistakes

- **Overstuffed lyrics.** Eight verses, the whole family tree, and three inside jokes in one song. A kid can handle two verses and a chorus; everything else is material for another song — and you can make as many of those as you want.
- **Endlessly fine-tuning pronunciation.** If the name doesn't come out clean even on the third try, don't keep fixing attempts four through ten — change the form of the name or the word in the lyrics instead. Five minutes in the lyrics saves an hour in the generator.
- **Playing the first result for the kids without listening first.** The generator occasionally sings something that isn't in the lyrics. Embarrassing for an adult; a real problem for a four-year-old at the cake. Always the whole thing, always beforehand.
- **Asking for the melody of a well-known song.** Doesn't work technically, and for modern songs not legally either — and the result disappoints more than a new melody would. A melody of its own is the whole point.
- **One generation and done.** The first attempt is rarely the best. Generate three or four variations and pick with your child — choosing is half the fun, and a kid who picked "their" version loves it twice as much.
- **Publishing a song with your child's name in it.** The nicest creations are the ones most tempting to share. Grandma, yes; social media, no.

## What you get out of it

This article, unusually, isn't counting hours saved — songs for kids don't save any hours, and that was never the job.

- **Joy, the main line item.** The look on a kid's face the first time they hear their own name in the chorus is the reason this guide exists. It's one of the rare cases where AI makes something you genuinely can't buy — a song about your child didn't exist in the world, and now it does.
- **Routines that actually work.** A tooth-brushing song and a cleanup song are the cheapest motivational tool a household with a preschooler will ever find: a ritual instead of a command. This is one place where you surprisingly do save something — your morning nerves.
- **Tradition.** The family anthem in the car, a birthday song every year. Things kids will remember their whole lives, made in fifteen minutes at the kitchen table.
- **Peace of mind.** The whole process is non-commercial, at home, and under your control: you read the lyrics, you heard the whole track, and the name never leaves the house. No gray area.

## Pro tip

Turn the birthday song into a **yearly tradition**. Ten minutes every year before the birthday: what the child mastered this year, what they're into, which form of their name is current — into the same prompt, a new song. By age six, your child has six songs, and you have an audio chronicle nobody else has: the song from age four sings about a balance bike, the one from age six about a bike with no training wheels. Set up a [project with persistent context](/tipy/ai-projekty-kontext) for this, where you save the lyrics from every year — Claude then keeps the style consistent and picks up from last year's chorus, so the songs sound like a series instead of random attempts. And someday, many years from now, it's an eighteenth-birthday gift that can't be wrapped.

The closing rule is the same as with everything you do with AI as a family: **the machine composes, the parent listens first, and the kids only after.** Everything else is just joy.
