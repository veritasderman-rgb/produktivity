The weekly review is the best-rated and worst-kept habit in productivity. The reason is simple: most of those thirty minutes don't go to thinking — they go to gathering. Clicking through the calendar, scanning the task list, digging through what's left sitting in your mail. Gathering is exactly what an automation can handle. What's left for you is the part nobody else can do: deciding what matters next week.

## A typical scenario

Radek, a team lead, has tried setting up a weekly review three times. Each time it lasted two weeks. At five on a Friday afternoon, half an hour of scrolling through the calendar sounds unbearable, so he tells himself he'll do it Sunday — and by Sunday he wants to do it even less. Monday then starts with whatever mail happens to throw at him.

With an automated review, a single page lands in his inbox at 2pm on Friday: what was on his calendar and where the time actually went, which tasks got finished, what's overdue, which emails nobody answered, and a proposed set of three priorities for next week with reasoning attached. Radek reads it, drops one suggested priority and swaps in another, confirms the other two. Done in five minutes — and Monday morning he already knows what he's doing.

## How to do it

1. **Decide what feeds the review.** Three sources are the minimum: calendar (what actually happened), task list (what's done and what's hanging), and mail (what's still waiting). More sources means a longer review, not a better one.
2. **Schedule it for Friday morning.** Not Friday evening. The review should land while you can still act on it — and while there's still time to follow up on a forgotten promise.
3. **Give it a fixed structure.** Five blocks work well: completed, overdue, waiting on others, where the time went, three proposed priorities for next week. Write the prompt as a template and add the instruction "always give a reason for each proposed priority."
4. **Keep proposals as proposals.** AI should suggest next week's priorities, not commit to them. Deciding what gets a week of your attention is management work, not something a machine does.
5. **Deliver the review wherever you'll actually open it.** An email to yourself, a Slack message, a Notion page. And block five minutes in your calendar right after it lands to go through it.
6. **Archive them.** Keep all your reviews in one place. After a quarter, they'll tell you more than any plan could — especially where your time keeps quietly disappearing.

## The best tools

- **Built-in AI in your existing environment (Gemini in Workspace, Copilot in Microsoft 365)** — the easiest start, calendar and mail access already sorted, running under your company account.
- **Zapier and Make** — scheduled runs, pulling data from your calendar and task list, an AI step, and delivery of the output — no coding required.
- **n8n** — the same role on your own server, when calendar and mail content shouldn't leave for a third-party service.
- **Todoist and Notion** — task managers with an open API and their own AI features; they double as both a data source and the place the review lives.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** 25 minutes a week, roughly twenty hours a year. But the time saved isn't the main win.
- **A habit that sticks:** a five-minute review survives even a hectic week. A thirty-minute one doesn't — which is exactly why it kept falling off.
- **Better decisions:** you pick priorities from the full picture, not from whatever happens to be sitting on top of your mind.
- **Calmer Mondays:** the week starts with your own plan, not with other people's demands from your inbox.

## Pro tip

Give the review one uncomfortable section: "tasks pushed back for the third time." A repeatedly postponed task usually isn't about time — it's badly defined, belongs to someone else, or you simply don't want to do it. That one list will save you more than any planning technique. On security: the review reads your mail and calendar, so build it on a company account with contractual data protection, exclude sensitive projects from the sources, and ask the prompt for summaries and links only — never a copy of the full messages.
