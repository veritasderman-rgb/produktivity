Most people use AI on their mail by hand: open a message, copy it into a chat, ask for a reply, paste the result back. It works, but the savings are measured in minutes. The real leverage is in a routine that runs on its own: **you open your inbox in the morning and every message waiting for a reply already has a draft ready** — in your tone, with dates pulled from your calendar. Your job shifts from writing to approving.

## A typical scenario

Petra, an operations manager, opens her inbox at 8:00 a.m. to 34 new messages. Nine need a reply, four of them variations on the same question ("when can you ship it?"). By 9:30 she's still writing emails, then heads into her first meeting feeling like she hasn't gotten anything done yet. She circles back to her inbox three more times before the day is over, worried she missed something.

With the routine turned on, the morning looks different: nine drafted replies are waiting in the Drafts folder. Six are usable as-is, two need a sentence tweaked, one she deletes and writes herself because it's a sensitive negotiation. By 8:20 she's done. AI never sent anything — she sent everything herself, she just didn't have to start from a blank page.

## How to do it

1. **Start with the simplest version.** AI assistants built right into mail — Gemini in Gmail, Copilot in Outlook — can suggest a reply with one click. Zero setup. The downside: a draft only gets generated once you open the message, so you're still starting from scratch each time.
2. **Write down your rules before you automate anything.** One page: how you sign off, what tone you use with clients versus colleagues, which types of messages don't get a reply at all (newsletters, notifications), what deadlines you're allowed to offer. This page is the engine of the whole routine — without it, you get generic, impersonal drafts.
3. **Build the automation.** In Zapier, Make, or n8n, create a flow: new message in a chosen folder → the message text and your rules go to an AI model → the output gets saved **as a draft** in your mailbox. Never as a sent message.
4. **Scope it down.** Don't run the routine across your whole inbox. Start with one category — say, messages tagged "needs reply," or mail from customers at a single address.
5. **The most powerful version: an agentic tool.** With a tool that has access to your email (Claude connected to your mail, for instance), you describe the rules once in plain language and it runs the routine repeatedly, including checking your calendar.
6. **Tune the rules for the first week.** Every correction you make to a draft, add as a sentence to the rules. After a week, accuracy will be noticeably higher.

## The best tools

- **Built-in AI in mail (Gemini in Gmail, Copilot in Outlook)** — the fastest start, runs on your company account, data stays in your work environment.
- **Zapier and Make** — cloud automation platforms with no coding; ideal for building the mail → AI → draft chain.
- **n8n** — a similar tool you can run on your own server; the choice for companies that want to keep their data in-house.
- **Agentic tools (Claude with email access, Claude Code)** — for anyone who'd rather describe the routine in sentences than assemble it step by step.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** conservatively 30–45 minutes a day. Reading and tweaking a draft takes a fraction of what writing from scratch does.
- **Peace of mind:** you don't start the morning with a blank page. The hardest part to get moving on disappears.
- **Quality:** the routine doesn't forget. No message you meant to answer "when there's time" is left hanging.
- **Consistency:** every reply carries the same tone and the same terms, because they all come from the same rules.

## Pro tip

Stick to one rule with no exceptions: **AI may prepare anything; only a human sends.** Never give the automation permission to send a message on its own — the difference between a draft and a sent message is the difference between an assistant and a liability. And watch your data: run the routine on a company account with contractual data protection, not a personal chat. Exclude folders with sensitive matters (HR, legal, health information) from the routine entirely, and once a quarter review exactly what the automation has access to — permissions have a way of quietly piling up.
