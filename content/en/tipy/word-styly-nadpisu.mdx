A document formatted by hand ("bold and bigger") is dead text. Styles turn it into structure. The difference between simply making a heading visually bigger and bold, versus actually assigning it a real Heading style, isn't obvious at first glance — it shows up the moment you need to navigate the document, reorder it, or generate a table of contents from it.

## A typical scenario

You're writing a longer paper with several chapters and subchapters. You format the headings by hand — select the text, enlarge the font, bold it, maybe underline it. It looks fine, until you need to reorganize the chapter order, quickly jump to a specific section of a thirty-page document, or generate a table of contents — none of which manually formatted text can do, because Word "doesn't know" it's a heading; it just sees big bold letters.

With styles like **Heading 1**, **Heading 2**, and others, Word knows what's what, and features open up that simply don't work with manual formatting: a navigation pane for quickly jumping between chapters, dragging entire chapters to a different spot in the document with one mouse move, and an automatic table of contents that updates itself.

## How to do it

1. Select the heading text and, on the **Home** tab in the styles gallery, choose **Heading 1** for main chapters, **Heading 2** for subchapters, and **Heading 3** for further divisions if needed. It's faster to do the same with the keyboard shortcuts <kbd>Ctrl</kbd> + <kbd>Alt</kbd> + <kbd>1</kbd>, <kbd>2</kbd>, or <kbd>3</kbd>.
2. Open **View → Navigation Pane** — an overview of all the headings in the document appears on the left, organized by level, letting you jump to any chapter with one click.
3. You can also drag headings around in the navigation pane — moving a heading moves the entire corresponding chapter along with the text beneath it, with no need to manually cut and paste text.
4. Go to **References → Table of Contents** and pick one of the automatic table-of-contents styles — Word builds a complete table of contents with page numbers from your existing heading styles.
5. Whenever the document changes (a chapter is added, the order changes, or pagination shifts), right-click the table of contents and choose **Update Field** — the table recalculates itself, with no need to retype it by hand.

## The best tools

- **Heading styles in Word (Heading 1/2/3)** — built in and free, the foundation for navigation, an automatic table of contents, and export to other formats.
- **Navigation Pane** — a built-in Word feature for quick orientation and reorganizing long documents without copying text.
- **Automatic Table of Contents (References → Table of Contents)** — a built-in feature that builds a table of contents from existing heading styles in seconds.
- **Google Docs (heading styles and document outline)** — a similar idea for people working in Google's environment, with the same logic of assigning heading levels.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** roughly 20 minutes on a longer document, where manually building and maintaining a table of contents, manually reordering chapters, and manually formatting every heading separately would take considerably longer.
- **Easy navigation:** the navigation pane lets you jump to any chapter without lengthy scrolling through the whole document.
- **Consistent appearance:** all headings of the same level look the same automatically, with no need to check you used the same size and boldness everywhere.
- **A professional impression:** a document with an automatic table of contents and a consistent heading structure looks far more organized to a reader or evaluator than manually formatted text.

## Pro tip

If you don't like the default look of the Heading styles (color, font, size), you don't need to go back to manual formatting — just adjust the style once (right-click the style in the gallery → **Modify**) and the change automatically applies to every heading of that level throughout the entire document.
