It's Thursday, half past seven in the evening. The activity schedule taped to the fridge is one nobody can read all the way through. The older kid wants to know why they can't sleep over at a friend's when everyone else is allowed to. The younger one is waiting in bed for a story, and the only thing on the parent's mind is that Saturday is a birthday party for which neither a present nor a game has been sorted out. None of this is a catastrophe on its own. Together, though, it's an evening that repeats five times a week and drains more energy than work does.

This guide covers four places where a language model genuinely helps: family logistics and the calendar, preparing for hard parenting moments, games and scavenger hunts for kids, and bedtime stories on demand. It isn't a guide to handing a child a chat window. It's a guide for the parent.

One sentence sits over the whole thing: **AI is a tool in a parent's hands — not a babysitter and not an educator.** The child never talks to the model directly; the parent is always in between. The model drafts the material — a schedule, a game, a cipher, a story — and the parent reads it, edits it, and only then passes it on. Everything else here is just that sentence worked out in practice.

You don't have to read this in one sitting. Each of the four sections stands on its own and comes with its own copy-paste prompts — just fill in the brackets. Need something for tonight? Skip to the bedtime stories. Planning a weekend with the kids? Start with the scavenger hunts.

## Four places where this makes sense

Parenting covers an enormous range of things, and AI doesn't touch most of it. It won't teach a child to ride a bike, won't calm them down at 3 a.m., and won't replace you being there. But four areas can be described concretely — each one works differently and carries different risks.

All four share one thing: none of the output is meant to go straight into a child's hands. A parent always stands between the model and the kid, reading it first. That sounds like a delay, but it's thirty seconds, and it's the only safeguard you have here.

## The family calendar and household logistics

Family logistics rarely fails because of one big problem — it fails because the information lives in seven different places. The activity schedule as a PDF from school, practice times in a group chat, the dentist scrawled on a scrap of paper in a wallet, parent-teacher conferences buried in an email — and who's driving whom on Tuesday exists only in one parent's head. A phone calendar doesn't fix this, because nobody ever actually sits down and retypes all of it by hand.

This is exactly where the model is useful, and boringly so: it reads a pile of mismatched inputs and turns them into one structured overview. A photo of the schedule from the bulletin board, text copied from a chat, a school PDF, a handwritten note — feed it all in, and it hands back a table you just need to check and drop into your calendar. There's a general tip on calendars, [A calendar that defends itself](/tipy/ai-kalendar-planovac); this is the family-specific version.

### Step one: turn chaos into one overview

Gather everything in one place — not by retyping it, but by photographing and copying it. Models routinely read images and scans, handwriting included.

One thing up front: if you're working with real names and school names, use a paid account whose provider contractually protects your data, and stay conservative even there. It's safer to write "older kid" and "younger kid" instead of names, and "Tuesday activity" instead of the name of the place. The overview works just as well, and nothing unnecessary goes anywhere.

```text
I'm a parent of two kids and want to build one overview of our family
logistics. I'm attaching several sources: a photo of the activity
schedule, copied messages from the parents' group chat, and text
from a school email.

Turn this into one table with these columns:
day, time from–to, who (older kid / younger kid / both), what,
location, who's driving, note.

Rules:
- Don't guess. If a time or location is missing from the source,
  write "missing" in the cell — don't fill in an estimate.
- At the end, add a list of questions I haven't answered that leave
  the overview incomplete.
- List recurring events (every Tuesday) once, not once per week.
```

It hands back a table and — more valuably — a list of gaps. You'll typically discover that you don't know when two activities end, and that one date shows up at two different times across two sources. Watch out: models like to fill gaps with a guess, which is why the prompt insists on "missing." Even so, check the source for every row before it goes into the calendar.

### Step two: a weekly plan you can actually live by

The full overview of every commitment is raw material. What a family actually needs on a Thursday evening is one sheet of paper on the fridge: what's happening tomorrow, who's driving whom, and what everyone needs to bring. Have it built fresh at the start of every week.

```text
From the attached family logistics overview, build a plan for next
week (Monday through Sunday) in a form I can pin to the fridge.

Format: one block per day, listed chronologically:
time — who — what — who's driving — what to bring.

Also:
- Start with a line called "Watch this week" listing at most three
  things that differ from a normal week.
- Where we need to be in two places at once, or where less than
  20 minutes separates two commitments, mark it "CONFLICT" and
  explain why.
- Don't add anything that isn't in the source. No advice, no tips.
- The whole thing has to fit on one page.
```

That last instruction matters: without it you'll get a plan padded with advice like "remember to get enough rest," which won't fit on one page and nobody reads anyway. Capping it at one page is the simplest way to force the model to cut.

The model catches conflicts more reliably than a tired person at ten at night, but not infallibly — it doesn't know how long the drive home from the pool takes unless you tell it. It's worth writing a couple of sentences into the source once, like "15 minutes on foot from school, 20 minutes by car to the activity including parking." From then on, the conflict warnings get noticeably more accurate.

### Step three: shared custody and handoffs

Families where kids move between two households carry an extra layer: the "what's where" list — swimsuit, sneakers, the math notebook, the charger, medication. There's nothing to invent here, only things to forget — which is exactly the kind of thing worth handing off to a system.

Use the second prompt from the previous step and add three things: how the alternating schedule works, where and when handoffs happen, and what needs to travel with the child every time versus only on certain weeks. Have the output split into "always packed" and "packed only during a [activity] week." And add one instruction: **write the summary for the other parent factually and neutrally — no judgment, no digs.** Without it, the model occasionally slips in a phrase that reads like a reproach, and that's the last thing you want in a handoff note. Read the whole thing before you send it: **AI drafts, the human sends.**

Household logistics as a system is the subject of [The household as an operation](/prirucka/domacnost-jako-provoz), and where parents' time actually goes is covered in [Parents and time](/prirucka/rodice-a-cas). ## Parenting: what AI is good for, and what it isn't

This is the section where it's easy to overshoot in either direction. One extreme is treating the chat as a hotline and taking its answers as expert advice. The other is dismissing the whole idea because "parenting isn't something you outsource to a computer." The useful spot is in between: **the model is good at preparation and phrasing, it is not good at diagnosis, and it never decides.**

### What it's good for

**Preparing for a hard conversation.** Most parenting conversations that go badly go badly because of the first thirty seconds. Thinking through how you'll open and what you don't want to say takes five minutes and pays for itself.

**Translating expert-speak into plain language.** A report from an educational psychologist, an assessment, a recommendation from school — text written by specialists for specialists. The model turns it into a readable summary and pulls out what to ask about. Careful: a translation is not a second opinion. Whatever's in the report stands; the model only changes the form.

**Working out how to explain something to a young child.** Why you can't dart across the street, why you can't have everything right now, why grandma can't come over. An adult explanation doesn't land with a six-year-old, and inventing an analogy on the spot, mid-question, is hard.

**Planning a habit change.** Bedtime, mornings, homework, tidying up. This isn't about a miracle method — it's about having a two-week plan written down instead of improvising every single evening.

**Getting more angles on something.** When a parent is stuck, what's usually missing isn't an answer but options. Asking for five different readings of what's going on is a way to unstick yourself from the one interpretation you're locked into. You still pick.

**Drafting questions for the doctor or for school.** Twenty minutes with the pediatrician or fifteen at parent-teacher conferences fly by, and half the things you meant to ask get forgotten. A list prepared beforehand is a trivial trick with an outsized payoff.

### What it's not good for

This list is shorter, but it's the one that's non-negotiable.

**The model is not a psychologist, a pediatrician, or an educator, and it must never stand in for one.** If you suspect a developmental issue, a learning difficulty, anxiety, depression, an eating disorder, or anything like it, the child belongs with a person — a pediatrician, a specialist, a psychologist. Not in a chat window. The model can write text that sounds like a professional opinion, and that's exactly the danger: a confident tone with no accountability and no actual look at the child. Use it to organize what you've observed so you can walk into that appointment prepared. Not to find out what's wrong with your child.

**The model knows nothing about your specific child.** It doesn't know their history, their temperament, their friends, or what happened at school last week. It answers with general patterns based on what you typed. If it suggests something that's obviously not going to work for your kid, it has no way of knowing that — but you do.

**The decision is always the parent's.** That holds even for trivial things. Ask whether a child should go to bed at eight or eight-thirty, and the model will answer, because it's built to answer. That doesn't mean it knows anything. Its output is a basis for thinking, not a verdict.

### What doesn't belong in the chat

Read this even if you skip the rest of the section. **A child's name, the name of their school or class, their address, date of birth, medical diagnoses, and photos of the child do not belong in an AI chat.** Not "just this once," not "it's harmless."

The fix costs nothing: write "my eight-year-old," "the younger kid," "school," "the teacher." The model doesn't need the names to do the work. Advice on "my eight-year-old has refused to get dressed in the morning for the last three weeks" is exactly the same advice as the version with a name in it.

When you genuinely can't avoid the topic — say you really do need to work through a medical report — two conditions apply. First, use a paid account whose provider contractually protects your data and doesn't train on your inputs. Second, anonymize the text before you paste it in anyway: strip the name, the ID number, the address, and the doctor's name. The rest of the report still makes sense without them. The broader context is covered in [AI, ethically and safely](/prirucka/ai-eticky-a-bezpecne).

### Prompts that model this way of working

Notice that the following prompts are anonymized and end with the parent deciding. That's not extra caution for its own sake — it forces the model to offer options instead of verdicts, which is also just better output.

```text
I'm preparing for a conversation with my [11-year-old] about
[talking unkindly about a classmate a lot lately]. I don't want
a lecture, and I don't want them to shut down right at the start.

Give me:
1. Three ways to open the conversation — each as one sentence I'll
   actually say out loud, with a note on what each one is likely
   to trigger (including the uncomfortable part).
2. Three sentences I should avoid, and why.
3. Four questions that will get me more information than if I do
   the talking.

Don't script the whole conversation, and don't guess what my child
will say — you don't know them. How I run it is my call.
```

It hands back usable opening lines and — more valuably — a warning about phrasings that would kill the conversation. Careful: the model likes to write long, therapy-sounding sentences nobody would ever say out loud. If one sounds like it's from a manual, cut it.

```text
I'm attaching text from a [report from an educational psychologist].
I've removed names, addresses, and other identifying details.

Give me:
1. A summary in plain English, ten sentences at most — what it says.
2. A glossary of the technical terms in the text, one sentence each.
3. Questions I should ask whoever wrote the report — especially
   anywhere the text is ambiguous or skips what to actually do.

Don't interpret beyond the text, don't add your own conclusions,
and don't tell me what you think about it. I want a translation,
not a second opinion.
```

It hands back a readable summary and a list of questions. Careful: if the model starts writing "this means your child probably...," it has gone off-script. Interpretation belongs to whoever actually evaluated the child.

For explaining things to young kids ("why you can't dart across the street," "why we can't have everything right now"), you don't need a long prompt. Just give the age and the topic, and ask for **three different explanations: one built on an analogy from a world the child already knows, one entirely plain in three sentences, and one framed as a question the child can answer for themselves** — plus, for each one, a note on where it's weak. Add a ban on scary framing and on referencing anything that supposedly happened to a specific real child. One of those three usually points you toward a fourth, your own version. The same applies to prepping for a doctor's visit or a parent-teacher conference: ask for eight questions ranked from most important, and explicitly forbid any guessing at causes — otherwise the model will run through a list of possible diagnoses that's useless before an appointment and only adds worry.

```text
I want to change my [9-year-old]'s evening routine. Right now it
looks like this: [description of what happens between dinner and
falling asleep, including where it gets stuck].
Goal: [in bed by 9 p.m. without a nightly negotiation].

Suggest a two-week plan:
- what to change right away versus what to save for week two,
- what should stay exactly the same every day (and why that
  specific thing),
- what to do if it stops working on day three,
- how I'll know after two weeks whether it worked.

Be concrete and realistic — don't tell me it's guaranteed to work.
And note anything in the plan that might be a bad idea specifically
for a child who [description of temperament — e.g., handles change
poorly].
```

It hands back a plan with a clear split between the fixed and flexible parts. The last paragraph of the prompt matters most — without it you get a generic plan; with it, a plan with the risks flagged. If it hasn't worked after two weeks, that's a signal to see a specialist, not to write another prompt.

## A turnkey scavenger hunt

Of all four areas, this is where the ratio of effort to payoff is best. A scavenger hunt is a game where kids move station to station, solving a task or cracking a cipher at each one that reveals where to go next, with a treasure at the end. Building one properly takes an hour and a half. With the model, a rough draft takes ten minutes, leaving time for the part only a parent can do: checking the ciphers, printing the cards, and hiding them.

One thing up front, so it doesn't get lost: **a scavenger hunt is paper, the yard, and a flashlight — not a tablet.** The model gets used during prep, not during play. No screens once the game starts.

### How a scavenger hunt works

The skeleton stays the same whether the theme is pirates or outer space.

How many stations? Around age five, three stations and twenty minutes; for seven-year-olds, four to five stations and forty minutes; for nine-year-olds, five to six stations and an hour; for eleven and up, six to eight stations and an hour and a half. Fewer stations and more delight at the treasure beats a chain the kids burn out on halfway through.

Two things decide whether it lands. First: the very first cipher has to be easy — if the kids can't crack anything right at the start, the game is over. Second: have a hint ready for every station. There's no shame in using it; the shame is standing around one piece of paper for twenty minutes.

### Ciphers and keys by age

A repertoire you can mix and match endlessly. For each one: roughly the right age, how long it takes to solve, and the thing that matters most — how to set it up.

**Picture substitution** (age 4+, 3–5 minutes). Each letter maps to a picture: sun, tree, fish. The station holds a message in pictures, with a "key" nearby — a table showing what stands for what. For the youngest kids, keep the message to three or four letters. Works even for kids who can't read yet.

**Mirror writing** (age 5+, 2–4 minutes). A message written backward and flipped, read by holding it up to a mirror or against a window from the other side. The easiest option for a first station; doing it by hand is fiddly, so it's simpler to flip the text in a text editor.

**Numbers for letters, A=1** (age 6+, 5–8 minutes). The most versatile cipher there is: A=1, B=2, up to Z=26. The word KEY is 11-5-25. Kids grasp it instantly, and it hides in plain sight — the numbers can live on a door, on clothespins, in a recipe.

**Caesar cipher** (age 8+, 10–15 minutes). Every letter shifts a fixed number of places through the alphabet: shifting by three turns TREASURE into WUHDVXUH. Kids need to get the shift amount from somewhere — the previous station, a count of stairs.

**Keyword cipher** (age 10+, 15–20 minutes). The cipher alphabet starts with a keyword and then continues with the remaining letters. With the keyword DECK it looks like this: D, E, C, K, A, B, F, G, H, I, J, L, M, N, O, P, Q, R, S, T, U, V, W, X, Y, Z. The word MAP encrypts to MDP. It has a weakness — letters from the middle of the alphabet onward stay the same — but that doesn't matter for a game.

**Morse code** (age 8+, 10–20 minutes). Dots and dashes. TREASURE is - / .-. / . / .- / ... / ..- / .-. / . The key has to be included — nobody has Morse memorized. Best not handed over on paper at all, but flashed with a flashlight through a window or tapped out — kids remember that for years. It takes a while to decode, so keep the message short.

**Invisible lemon-juice ink** (age 6+, 5 minutes plus prep). Text written with a cotton swab dipped in lemon juice is invisible once dry and appears when the paper is heated. An adult does the heating, not the child. Test it a day ahead — it doesn't work on every paper.

**Hidden message in a book** (age 9+, 10–15 minutes). The key is a set of number triples: page, line, word. "42-7-3, 42-9-1, 18-2-6" are three words from a book you have at home. Easy to set up, but check every single word against your own copy — page numbers vary by edition.

**QR code** (age 8+, 2 minutes). A printed code that shows a sentence or a coordinate when scanned. Kids love it, but it's the one place a phone enters the game — at most once per hunt, and ideally the parent does the scanning.

**Cut-up paper puzzle** (age 4+, 5–10 minutes). Print the message, cut it into fifteen pieces, and scatter them around the station. It's not a cipher — it's patience — and it's the best task for mixed-age groups, where the older kids decode and the younger ones assemble.

**UV pen** (age 6+, 3 minutes). A pen with invisible ink and a UV light built into the cap. A cheap little gadget that turns a hunt into an event. Works best in dim light, so it suits an evening or basement station.

There's one rule for sequencing them: **easiest cipher first, hardest in the middle, quick one last.** By that point the kids can already see the treasure, and patience is gone.

### How to check a cipher before you hand it to the kids

This is the single most important paragraph in the whole section — the place where scavenger hunts most often fall apart.

**Models make mistakes shifting letters mechanically.** They're excellent at inventing a story and stations. Working out that T shifted by three becomes W is, surprisingly, something they're bad at — they process text in chunks, not letter by letter, and stumble on longer words. The same problem shows up with Morse code, number ciphers, and keyword ciphers. You'll find out either at home while checking, or in the yard, when the kids are stuck on a cipher that has no solution.

The fix is tedious and takes five minutes:

- **Decode the ciphertext back yourself.** Not by asking the model whether it checks out — it will confidently say yes regardless. Pencil on paper, letter by letter.
- **For number and Caesar ciphers, write the alphabet down the margin of the page and number it.** Checking then takes about a minute.
- **For Morse code, check the separators.** The model often forgets to separate letters and words, producing one unbroken string of dots that can't be decoded.
- **For a book cipher, look every word up in your own copy.** The model doesn't have your edition and made the numbers up.
- **Check what the message actually says, too.** If the decoded message reads "behind the washing machine," confirm there really is something behind the washing machine and that the child can reach it.

You can speed up the check by having the model list the solutions and keys separately. But never hand those over unedited — checking it back by hand yourself is the only option.

### Six ready-made scavenger hunts

Each one has its own theme, setting, and mood. Use them as-is, or as a template — and above all, use the prompt attached to each one, which rewrites it for your own home and neighborhood.

#### 1. The quest for the ring

**Age:** 7–11. **Where:** the home and yard. **How long:** 60–75 minutes.

The frame: a letter arrives that morning from an old traveler — a ring hidden in the house must not fall into the wrong hands, and the path to a safe place runs through four stops on an old map. Each stop reveals only a fragment, so all four have to be found.

- **At the gate:** assemble a torn map from eight pieces. On the assembled map, the next location is written in mirror writing.
- **By the water:** a message written in A=1 numbers. The key is a count of stairs or steps the kids have to measure out nearby.
- **In the dark:** a message in invisible ink or UV pen, paired with a flashlight hidden at the previous station.
- **At the edge:** a Caesar cipher with a shift the kids get from a number found on the map.

Reward: a string ring for each child, plus something sweet, ideally in a small wooden box tied with string.

```text
Design a scavenger hunt for [3] kids ages [8, 9, and 11] on a fantasy
theme with a ring and a map.

Setting: [our house — description of layout — and the backyard, plus
a patch of woods past the fence]. Time: [70 minutes]. Stations: [4].

For each station, tell me: exactly where to hide the card (three
options based on my description), the text on the card including
a story sentence, the type of cipher and the complete key, the
solution, and a hint in case the kids get stuck.

Use these ciphers: cut-up paper puzzle, mirror writing, numbers for
letters (A=1), Caesar cipher. Keep them short, two words max.

At the end, add an opening letter (5 sentences max) I'll read to the
kids, a list of things to prepare, and a table with the solution to
every cipher.
```

It hands back a full scenario, cards and all. Careful: decode the ciphers back yourself, and adjust the hiding spots to match reality — the model doesn't know your house, only your description of it.

#### 2. Pirate treasure

**Age:** 5–8. **Where:** the yard or a park. **How long:** 30–40 minutes.

The frame: a bottle holds a note from a captain who buried a treasure and tore up the map so nobody could find it whole. Whoever collects every piece reaches the chest.

- **First:** picture substitution — three symbols with the key drawn beside them. It spells out the name of a spot nearby ("the tree," "the bench").
- **Second:** a physical task — carry "water" in a cup, hop across stepping stones. Completing it earns the next map piece.
- **Third:** A=1 numbers with the key included, a short three-letter word.

Reward: a chest (a shoebox wrapped in paper) with chocolate coins, bandanas, and paper medals.

```text
Design a simple pirate scavenger hunt for [4] kids ages [5 to 7] —
two of them can't read well yet.

Setting: [a yard with a sandbox, two trees, and a shed]. Time:
[35 minutes]. Stations: [3].

Conditions:
- At least one station must be solvable without reading (pictures,
  counting, a physical task).
- Ciphers no longer than three letters, each with a printable key.
- For each station, tell me what to do if the kids get stuck.

Add the text of the message in the bottle (4 sentences, simple
words), a list of props, and a table with the solution to every
cipher.
```

It hands back a game suited to preschoolers. Careful: models tend to write text for young kids at too high a level — read it out loud yourself, and if you hesitate on a sentence, the child won't follow it either.

#### 3. The detective case of the missing thing

**Age:** 8–12. **Where:** indoors. **How long:** 45–60 minutes.

The frame: a [cake / set of keys / stuffed animal] has vanished from the kitchen without a trace, and four clues were accidentally left behind. The task: piece together who did it and where it's hidden.

- **First:** a list of suspects with elimination questions — each answer rules one out. A logic task, not a cipher.
- **Second:** a book cipher, three page–line–word triples. The book is on the shelf and marked.
- **Third:** a keyword cipher, with the key coming from the first clue (the culprit's name).
- **Fourth:** a short Caesar cipher revealing the hiding spot.

Reward: the missing item, plus a "detective badge" with a stamp.

```text
Design an indoor detective scavenger hunt for [2] kids ages [9 and 12].
[The cake we baked for tomorrow's party] has gone missing.

Setting: [description of the home, room by room: ...]. Time:
[50 minutes]. Stations: [4].

I want an investigation, not a chase:
- the first clue should be a logic puzzle with a list of [5] suspects
  and elimination clues (invented characters, not family members),
- the second clue a book cipher (page–line–word) — explain how I
  should check the numbers against my own copy,
- the third clue a keyword cipher, keyed to the culprit's name,
- the fourth a short Caesar cipher pointing to the hiding spot.

For each clue: the card text, the solution, and a hint.
At the end, a reveal I can read out to the kids.
```

It hands back a story with logic that holds together. Careful: the first clue has to actually be solvable — ask for a table showing that the clues rule out everyone except one suspect. Models sometimes leave two possible solutions, or none.

#### 4. Space mission

**Age:** 6–10. **Where:** indoors, ideally in the evening. **How long:** 45–50 minutes.

The frame: a distress signal arrives overnight from a ship that's lost its course. The crew needs four pieces of a navigation code to get home — one on each deck, and the clock is running.

- **Engine room:** Morse code tapped on a radiator or flashed with a flashlight, key included on the card. A short word.
- **Lab:** A=1 numbers written on cups or clothespins that the kids have to put in order.
- **Storage:** a QR code that reveals a coordinate ("third shelf from the left, bottom").
- **Bridge:** assemble the code from the pieces collected and "enter" it — write it on paper and drop it in a box.

Reward: glow-in-the-dark stars for the ceiling, or a trip to the planetarium.

```text
Design an indoor space-mission scavenger hunt for [2] kids ages
[6 and 9]. We'll play after dark and can turn the lights off.

Setting: [the home, room by room: ...]. Time: [45 minutes].
Stations: [4].

Use these ciphers: Morse code (tapped or flashed — explain how to
send it and how long the pauses should be; 4 letters max, and I'll
include the key), numbers for letters, a QR code (just give me the
text to put into the code), and a final step assembling the code
from the pieces.

For each station: card text written like a shipboard announcement,
the cipher, the solution, a hint, and an idea for where to hide it.
Add a "ship's log" opening (5 sentences max) and a solution table.
```

It hands back a game that works far better in the dark than in the afternoon. Careful: check the Morse code against a reference table — it's the cipher the model gets wrong most often — and make sure the pauses between letters are clearly marked.

#### 5. Forest trail hunt

**Age:** 6–12. **Where:** woods or a park. **How long:** 75–90 minutes.

The frame: an expedition passed through the forest but never made it to the end, leaving markers and a box behind. Whoever can read the trail gets where they didn't.

- **First:** arrows made of sticks and pinecones — the kids learn the markers and follow them to a note in a plastic bag.
- **Second:** a nature task — find five different leaves, three pinecones, something smooth, and something prickly. Completing it earns part of the key.
- **Third:** a mirror-writing message tied to a tree, read against the surface of a stream or a shiny lid.
- **Fourth:** a Caesar cipher pointing to the box's location, with the shift equal to the number of pinecones collected.

Reward: a box under a rock — a notebook, a pencil, and marshmallows for a fire, or plans for where to roast them.

```text
Design a forest trail scavenger hunt for [5] kids ages [6 to 12]
(a mixed group — the youngest can't read well).

Setting: [mixed woods with a path, a stream, and a large rock, route
about 2 kilometers]. Time: [80 minutes]. Stations: [4].

Conditions:
- The materials have to survive two hours outdoors — tell me what
  to put in a bag and what to do if it rains.
- One station should be a purely natural collecting task with no
  reading, so the youngest can do it too.
- Nothing gets left in the forest — add a list of things to collect.
- Ciphers: mirror writing and a Caesar cipher, both short.

For each station: text, cipher, solution, hint, and how to mark it
so the kids can find it but passersby can't.
```

It hands back a route with logistics included. Careful: the model doesn't know your woods — treat the location descriptions as suggestions and walk the route yourself beforehand; twenty minutes of scouting a day ahead saves an hour of wandering with the kids.

#### 6. A rainy-day indoor hunt

**Age:** 8–13. **Where:** the home and the building's hallway. **How long:** 40–50 minutes.

The frame: an old map has survived, showing places that look different today, and the only way to make sense of it is the notes left by a previous searcher. It's raining, but everything needed is inside.

- **First:** a keyword cipher, keyed to the searcher's last name printed on the envelope.
- **Second:** find five things in the home older than the child and put them in order of age. Completing it earns the next card.
- **Third:** a book cipher (page–line–word) from a book on the shelf.
- **Fourth:** A=1 numbers hidden somewhere numbers naturally appear — on doorbells, on doors, on a calendar.

Reward: a "searcher's license" on card stock, and an agreement that the kids design the next hunt for you.

```text
Design an indoor scavenger hunt for a rainy afternoon, for [3] kids
ages [8 to 13].

Setting: [a small apartment, plus the shared hallway with doorbells
and a stairwell]. Time: [45 minutes]. Stations: [4].

Use things that are normally around the home — bookshelf, calendar,
door numbers, old photos. No props I'd have to go out and buy.

Ciphers: keyword cipher, book cipher (page–line–word), and numbers
for letters. Include the full key and solution for each.

Add an opening text for the envelope (4 sentences max), a prep list,
and a version in case we can't use the hallway (everything happens
inside the apartment).
```

It hands back a game that needs nothing extra. Careful: check the book-cipher numbers against your own copy — the model is guessing at them. And once the game's over, take down anything you taped up in the shared hallway.

### How to get a hunt custom-tailored

The six above are a starting line. The real value is having a game built in five minutes that fits your home, your neighborhood, and whatever the kids are into right now. Use this prompt every time — just change the brackets.

```text
You're an experienced organizer of kids' games. Design me a
turnkey scavenger hunt.

Details:
- Number of kids: [3]
- Ages: [6, 8, and 11] — has to keep both the youngest and the
  oldest engaged
- Space: [describe the home, yard, park, or route — be specific:
  rooms, furniture, trees, stairs, landmarks]
- Time: [60 minutes]
- Theme: [whatever the kids are into right now]
- Treasure at the end: [what it'll be]
- What I have on hand: [printer, flashlight, UV pen, string...]
- What I don't want: [scary elements, running on stairs, anything
  breakable]

Output structure:
1. An opening text I'll read to the kids (5 sentences max).
2. A station table: number, location, task type, cipher, solution.
3. For each station: card text, the cipher's key, the solution,
   a stuck-hint, and an idea for where to hide it.
4. A list of everything I need to prepare, with an estimated
   prep time.
5. A backup plan: what to do if the game is too hard, and if it's
   going too fast.

Rules:
- Easiest cipher first, hardest in the middle, quick one last.
- Mix up the task types: cipher, physical task, search, question.
- The youngest kid needs something to do at every single station.
- Keep ciphers short — two words max.
- List the solution to every cipher separately, so I can check it.
```

Once the scenario is right, stay in the same thread and ask for a **print-ready version** — the step most parents skip and then end up retyping cards by hand. Ask: "Turn this into print-ready materials: cards, one per station, separated by a cut line, showing only what the kids should see — the story sentence and the cipher, no solutions. Cipher keys as separate cards. The opening letter on its own page for the envelope. And at the end, a parent sheet with a map of where everything's hidden, the solutions, the hints, and a prep checklist. Format it as plain text, with large type on the kids' cards."

Before you send it to the printer, check one thing: **that the solution didn't accidentally end up on a kid's card.** It happens, and it ends the game. And the rule still stands — you have to decode the ciphers back yourself.

One last and very useful thing: **the theme can be swapped out anytime.** There's a single line in the prompt, "theme," and everything else stays put. Whether your kids are into dinosaurs this year, a soccer league, or a show you've never heard of, the process is the same — write whatever your kid tells you into that line, and add two sentences: what that world is called, who the main characters are, and what the goal is. If you don't know the theme either, ask the child and write it in their own words. The model fills in the rest, and when it gets a detail wrong, the kids will correct it — and enjoy doing so.

A note on the IP side of things: using a favorite movie or book world for a home game with your own kids is fine, the same way telling your child a bedtime story about characters from a book is fine. What's not fine is publishing that game, selling it, or turning it into a paid event. For an afternoon in the backyard, none of that changes anything in practice.

## Bedtime stories on demand

The idea is simple. The child says what the story should be about — pure nonsense is fine, "about a digger that's afraid of rain," "about a cat who became postmaster" — and the model writes a story in under a minute. **The parent reads it first, and only then reads it aloud to the child.**

That order isn't a courtesy — it's the entire point of this section. The model occasionally drifts toward something you don't want in a bedtime story: a scary scene, a character's death described literally, a moralizing ending that echoes the exact argument you had at home an hour earlier. It's not being malicious — classic fairy-tale patterns contain these things, and the model imitates them. Skim it in thirty seconds and you'll catch it. Start reading straight off the screen and you'll find out mid-sentence.

Practical tip: generate the story in the afternoon, not at nine at night. That gives you time to read it and cut what doesn't fit. In the evening, all you do is read.

### A basic custom bedtime story

```text
Write a bedtime story for a child age [5].
Topic, chosen by the child: [a digger that's afraid of rain].

Conditions:
- Length for 5 minutes of reading aloud, roughly 700 words.
- Calm pacing, a comforting ending — the child should end up sleepy
  after it, not wound up.
- Nobody dies, nobody gets lost for long, no scary characters.
- Short sentences, concrete words, no abstract concepts.
- One repeating line the child can look forward to.
- No moral at the end. If something is implied, let it come from
  the story, not a stated lesson.

Write only the story, no introduction or explanation.
```

It hands back roughly a five-minute read. Careful: the most common slip-up is a moralizing final paragraph along the lines of "and so Digger the digger learned that..." Even with the instruction, the model adds one sometimes — cut it, the story works better without it. The second common issue is long, run-on sentences; if you find yourself out of breath reading aloud, ask for it to be rewritten in shorter ones.

### The child as the hero, with a sequel the next night

The strongest version is the one where the child is the hero. The rule from the parenting section applies here without exception: **no real name.** Use a nickname the child makes up. The child won't mind — they usually enjoy it more — and you're not putting their identity into a chat.

```text
Write a story where the main character is named [Pinecone] — a
nickname my [6-year-old] made up.

What I know about Pinecone: [brave, loves dogs, afraid of elevators].
Tonight's topic: [an expedition into the backyard, where statues
come alive at night].

Conditions:
- About 700 words, 5 minutes of reading aloud.
- Pinecone solves the problem alone — no adult swoops in to help.
- Leave the ending open: the last paragraph should hint at where
  they'll head tomorrow, without giving anything away.
- At the very end (outside the story itself), give me a list of
  the characters, places, and things that appeared, so I can build
  on them tomorrow.
```

It hands back a story plus an inventory of the world. That inventory is what turns a one-off story into a series — paste it back in the second night and the sequel picks up where it left off. Careful: an open ending sometimes gets a child excited instead of settled; if that happens, ask for a closed ending next time and start the following installment fresh.

You request a sequel by writing, in the same thread, what tomorrow's chapter should be about. It's more practical to set this up as a standing project with persistent context — store the character and world inventory in it, and start every evening with the model already knowing who's who, as described in [Projects in AI](/tipy/ai-projekty-kontext). Without a project, a note in your phone works fine too — just copy the relevant part in.

### A story that quietly deals with a real topic

Parents usually discover this version after a few evenings, and it's the most useful thing in the whole section. When what's going on at home is fear of the dark, a new sibling, moving house, or a pet's death, talking about it directly usually doesn't work. A story where someone else goes through the same thing works better — and that's exactly what bedtime stories have always done.

```text
Write a story for a [4-year-old] that quietly touches on this topic:
[we're having a second baby in two months, and the older one keeps
asking whether we'll still love them the same].

Conditions:
- Don't name the topic directly. No sibling appears in the story —
  it has to work through an image, not a matching situation.
- Give the hero a moment of fear, and let them discover they haven't
  lost anything.
- No moral, no sentence like "and so they realized that..." The
  child should take something from it on their own, or not, and
  that's fine either way.
- About 600 words, calm tone, a reassuring ending.

Below the story, add two sentences on what image you used to carry
the topic — so I know what I'm actually reading to my child.
```

It hands back a story that approaches the topic sideways. Careful: models have a strong pull toward a didactic ending on prompts like this, which undoes the whole effect — cut the last paragraph if it's there. And if the story ever feels too close to home for your own child, don't read it: you know what they can handle, the model doesn't.

### A story based on a drawing your child made

```text
I'm attaching a photo of a drawing my [5-year-old] made.

First, describe what you see in the picture — just a plain list,
no interpretation, no judgment of the drawing. Then wait; I'll
confirm it or correct it.

Once I confirm it, write a 5-minute bedtime story that includes
everything we confirmed, and nothing extra. Give the characters
names that fit the drawing.
Calm tone, comforting ending, no moral.
```

It hands back a description first, and only then the story. That middle step matters — models read children's drawings unreliably, and if it turns a purple blob into a monster, your child will notice while you're reading and be disappointed. Confirm the description first (or correct it: "that's not a monster, that's mommy") and the story lands. It's the loveliest version in the whole section, because the child hears their own drawing come back as a story. Working with photos from a phone is covered in [Snap a photo and it's a task](/tipy/foto-do-akce-claude-mobil).

### What AI can't replace here

One honest note to close on. **What's valuable about a bedtime story isn't the text — it's the parent reading it.** The lap, the voice, the fact that someone stops doing everything else and is just there for twenty minutes. The model saves the work of inventing something, which is a real relief for a worn-out parent at nine at night, and it's often the reason the bedtime story happens at all. That's its entire contribution, and it's a lot.

Having a synthetic voice read the story instead of you is a different thing. It's technically possible and sometimes has its place — a long car ride, a parent away for work, a lost voice. But it isn't the same, and there's no point pretending it is. Everyone can weigh that trade-off for themselves — just do it deliberately, not as something that gets offered once as more convenient and quietly becomes the habit a month later.

## What to watch out for

A summary of the rules that hold across all four areas.

- **The child doesn't talk to the model alone.** Not because something terrible is likely, but because the model is built to sound convincing and the child has no way to weigh that. With older kids, a point will come where this stops applying — but let that be your decision, and have a conversation about it first. More detail in [Digital hygiene for the family](/prirucka/digitalni-hygiena-rodiny).
- **No personal data.** Name, school, address, date of birth, diagnoses, photos. An anonymized description gets you an equally good result. Anything sensitive stays in a paid account with contractual data protection.
- **The parent reads and approves everything that reaches the child.** The story, the card, the cipher, the explanation. No exceptions. It's the only check that exists anywhere in this whole chain.
- **The model makes things up.** For kids' questions, that has a concrete cost: if you ask it to explain why the sky is blue or how a volcano works, read it and verify anything you're unsure of. For homework and school reports, that goes double — get facts from another source. The approach is covered in [AI makes things up with total confidence. Verify it](/tipy/ai-overovani-faktu).
- **Check the mechanical stuff by hand.** Ciphers, letter shifts, Morse code, page numbers. The model is unreliable here, and asking "is this correct?" won't help — it will say yes.
- **Screens have a ceiling, and the game lives outside them.** The model gets used during quiet prep. The scavenger hunt itself runs on paper, a flashlight, and pinecones. The moment it turns into a game on a tablet, it's a different activity, and the best part of it is gone.
- **Don't pretend to a child that someone else wrote it.** If they ask where the story came from, tell them. An eight-year-old can handle that, and it's a better opening for a conversation about these tools than any lecture would be.

## The most common mistakes

- **Handing a child the chat and walking away.** The model isn't supervision, and its defining trait — that it always answers, confidently — is a risk around a child, not a benefit.
- **Using a cipher without checking it.** It ends with kids in the woods stuck on a cipher that has no solution, and the game can't be saved. Five minutes of checking at the table is the cheapest insurance in this whole article.
- **Reading a bedtime story straight off the screen.** Skip the read-through and you're gambling on a scene you don't want to deal with at half past eight at night. Generate it in the afternoon, read it, cut it, read it aloud in the evening.
- **Asking the model for a diagnosis.** It will answer, and it'll sound authoritative. It won't be useful, and at worst it delays a visit to someone who can actually see the child.
- **Typing a name and school into the chat.** A habit that forms in three seconds and is hard to undo. Set "older kid" and "younger kid" as the default from day one and you'll never have to think about it again.
- **Making the scavenger hunt too long.** Eight stations sounds great while planning and, in practice, means the kids check out by station six. Go with four, and leave time to actually enjoy the treasure.
- **Forgetting the youngest kid in the group.** If a three-year-old just stands there at every station, the game is a punishment for them. That's exactly why the prompt includes a line requiring something for the youngest to do at every stop — don't drop it.

## What you get out of it

**Time.** Consolidating family logistics costs two hours once, then ten minutes a week for the weekly plan; compared to forwarding messages around and hunting down dates, that's usually an hour or two saved a week. A scavenger hunt that used to take an hour and a half now takes about twenty minutes, half of that printing and hiding. A bedtime story goes from twenty minutes of scrambling for ideas to about one minute.

**Peace of mind.** The main takeaway of this whole article. The gap between "I forgot something and I don't know what" and one sheet on the fridge isn't measured in minutes — it's measured in how much a person is carrying in their head. Same with a hard conversation: having the first three sentences worked out ahead of time makes for a different evening than improvising in the heat of the moment.

**Quality.** The scavenger hunt a parent wouldn't have had the energy for on a Friday night actually happens. Not because the model dreams up a better one — but because without it, there wouldn't be one at all.

**What it doesn't get you.** It doesn't shrink the number of hours you spend with your kids, and it isn't meant to. It doesn't replace a specialist. And it doesn't solve any of the genuinely hard parts of family life — it just clears away the mechanics around them, so there's more room left for the hard part.

## Pro tip

Once a year, build yourself a "family file" — one text document with an anonymized description of your household: how many kids and what ages, what they're into, what scares them, what your home and neighborhood look like, what props you have lying around, how long the usual commutes take. No names, no addresses. Attach that file to every prompt in this article.

The difference is bigger than you'd expect: the model stops giving generic suggestions and starts working with the fact that you have a narrow hallway, two kids five years apart, and a yard with exactly one tree. The most comfortable setup is keeping it in a project with persistent context, where it attaches itself automatically; but it works just as well as a note in your phone that you copy a chunk from each time.

And one closing rule that outlasts every tool: **the model prepares, the parent decides and reads.** The card, the cipher, the story, the sentence in a hard conversation. If you take one thing from this article, make it that — everything else here is just prompts you can come back to when you need them.
