The reality is simple: the tools exist, and students use them. The line between cheating and craft isn't whether you turn AI on — it's **who owns the thinking**. Cheating is letting AI write a thesis you can't defend. Craft is letting it handle the mechanics — sorting sources, scaffolding scripts, typesetting — while the arguments, the analysis, and the conclusions are yours. This workflow is the second path.

## A typical scenario

Tom is writing his bachelor's thesis on consumer behavior. He has forty sources: scanned PDFs, websites, three of his own datasets from a survey. The classic approach would mean a month of reading and note-taking, a week fighting with Excel, and a week wrestling a Word template.

Instead, he uploads the sources into a tool that only answers from them. By the end of one evening he has a map of the literature: who claims what, where the authors disagree, what his topic already covers, and where the gap is that he can fill. He doesn't run the survey analysis in a chat window — he has a script written that crunches the numbers from his CSV. He writes the text himself, chapter by chapter, in a single file with citations. Before submitting, he has a simulated opponent tear the thesis apart — and at the defense he's calm, because he can explain every sentence.

## How to do it

1. **Rules first, tools second.** Every university has (or is right now writing) a policy on AI: what's allowed, what has to be disclosed, what gets you disqualified. Read it, and disclose your AI use exactly as it requires. This step can't be skipped — everything else rests on it.
2. **Sources into NotebookLM.** Upload everything: scans, PDFs, web pages, notes, the assignment brief. The tool's strength is that it answers **only from your sources** and shows where each answer came from. Have it produce summaries of individual sources, comparisons across authors, and a map of themes — that's weeks of literature review compressed into an evening.
3. **Keep the outline and prep in a single .md file.** In Claude Cowork, have it draft a structure from your material and keep the whole text in one markdown file, citations included next to every claim. One file, one source of truth: your text and your citations never drift out of sync.
4. **AI never does the math in chat — a script does.** A language model isn't a calculator: looking at a long table, it only sees a slice of its context window and estimates numbers "by eye." The right move is to have it **write a Python script** that runs the analysis on your CSV — the results are exact, reproducible, and every step can be shown. Have the script export the charts straight to PNG, too.
5. **Typesetting comes last.** Move the finished markdown into whatever tool you're formatting in (Affinity, Word, LaTeX) — parts of the conversion can be automated, and Affinity can even be driven through a Claude Cowork connection. Then comes the manual work that turns a draft into your thesis: rewrite it in your own voice, strip out AI-sounding phrasing, and **check every citation against the original** — language models invent sources, and one citation that doesn't exist hurts more at your defense than a weak chapter.
6. **A simulated opponent before you submit.** Hand the finished text to a different AI than the one you wrote with, and give it a role: "you're a strict thesis advisor — find the weak points in the methodology, the argument, and the structure." Fix, repeat. Then you walk into the real defense with a thesis that's already survived one round of opposition.

## The best tools

- **[NotebookLM](https://notebooklm.google.com)** — research anchored to your own sources: it answers only from the documents you upload and cites where it got each answer.
- **Claude Cowork** — drafts content from a folder of source material, writes analysis scripts, and, through MCP, can drive other apps during typesetting.
- **Python (pandas, matplotlib)** — scripts for analyzing CSVs and exporting charts; you don't need to be able to write them, but you need to understand them well enough to defend them.
- **A second model as opponent** — ChatGPT or another AI you didn't write with: fresh eyes catch what a "co-author" tends to miss.

## What you get out of it

- **Time to think:** weeks of mechanics (note-taking, retyping, formatting) shrink to days — and the time you get back goes into the argument that actually makes the thesis good.
- **Confidence in your data:** analysis run by a script on your CSV is exact and reproducible — no numbers eyeballed in a chat window.
- **Calm at the defense:** the workflow forces you to own every claim; a simulated opposition means you've already heard the hardest questions once.

## Pro tip

One rule sits above every tool: **nothing goes into the thesis that you can't defend without AI in the room.** Learning by doing still matters — for the parts you're supposed to learn from, do them by hand even when the tool could do them faster. And watch your data: anonymize survey data with personal information, and only put it into AI through a paid account with contractual data protection, never through a free chat.
