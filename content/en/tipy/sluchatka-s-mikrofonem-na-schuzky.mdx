On video calls, everyone worries about the camera, but sound decides the impression you leave. The other side will forgive a grainy picture; a voice that booms like it's coming from a bathroom, gets buried under keyboard clatter, or fades in and out every time you turn your head wears listeners down every single minute of the call. That's because a laptop's built-in microphone picks up the whole room from half a meter away — echo and all, and everything else happening in it. The fix is unglamorous: any headphones with a microphone, even the plain ones sitting in a drawer. A mic a few centimeters from your mouth picks up your voice, not the room.

## How to do it

1. Use whatever you have: wired phone earbuds with an inline mic, wireless earbuds, a headset. Anything with a mic close to your mouth beats a laptop's built-in one.
2. In your meeting app, confirm they're actually being used — in audio settings, select the headphones as both **microphone and speaker**. Systems sometimes leave the laptop's mic active after you plug something in.
3. Listen to yourself: Teams has a **Make a test call** option under **Settings → Devices** that plays your recording back to you. Elsewhere, a plain voice-memo app does the job — thirty seconds of recording tells you more about your sound than a year of meetings.
4. Wearing headphones also solves the other half of the problem: sound from speakers feeding back into the mic, so the other side hears an echo of themselves. With headphones, that echo disappears.
5. If you talk to clients daily, consider a headset with a boom mic — it holds the microphone at the same distance no matter how you move, so your voice level doesn't waver.

## A typical scenario

Instructor Petra runs online courses from her living room. Participants complained they could barely understand her, so she bought a better webcam — it didn't help, because the problem was the laptop's microphone a meter away, picking up the echo of a tall room. A plain headset fixed it in a minute: her voice suddenly sounds full and close, the keyboard stopped hammering into the call, and Petra can stand up and walk around while lecturing without “leaving” acoustically too.

## What you get out of it

People understand you the first time, so you repeat yourself less and meetings don't drag on unnecessarily. And because listening to a clean voice is far less tiring than deciphering a booming one, the other side stays attentive longer — which matters for presentations, teaching, and negotiations alike.

## Pro tip

Software background-noise suppression is built into Teams, Zoom, and Meet today, and plenty of headphones have mic-side noise suppression too — they'll filter out a lawnmower outside or a barking dog. But software can't work miracles on echo and distance from your mouth — the foundation is a microphone that's simply close.
