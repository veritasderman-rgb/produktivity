A to-do list says what. A calendar says when. Without a “when,” the “what” often never happens at all — a task can sit on a list for a week or more, simply because no other commitment claims a specific time slot in its place. Timeboxing solves this problem in a simple way: it gives every task its own slot in the calendar, exactly the way a meeting has one.

## A typical scenario

A manager has eight items on today's task list. Without timeboxing, the day turns into hopping between emails, meetings, and randomly picking from the list based on whatever comes to mind — and by evening, the three most important tasks somehow didn't fit into the day, even though “the whole day was supposedly free for them.” What happened is what always happens: unclaimed time got filled by other, less important but more urgent-looking things.

With timeboxing, the same day looks different: in the morning, the three main tasks each get a concrete block — 9:00 to 10:30 for preparing a proposal, 11:00 to 12:00 for client calls. The calendar is now just as full as the task list used to be, but this time you can see exactly when each thing happens — and what simply won't fit into the day is clear first thing in the morning, not by evening.

## How to do it

1. In the morning, take the three main tasks of the day (ideally chosen ahead of time — see the tip on picking your three main tasks) and give them **specific blocks in your calendar** — not a vague “morning,” but an exact start and end time.
2. Set a rule: one block equals one task. Resist the urge to cram several things into one slot — timeboxing works precisely because each item has a clearly defined space.
3. Estimate a realistic duration for the task and add roughly **25% extra buffer**. People almost always underestimate how long work actually takes, and a block with no buffer falls apart at the first small complication.
4. Whatever doesn't fit into the day, deliberately move to a specific other time — not with a vague “I'll probably get to it,” but write down “Tuesday 9:00” and give it a block there. A vague postponement means the task keeps sliding indefinitely.
5. Leave short buffers (5 to 10 minutes) between blocks to transition between tasks and handle small things that come up in between — without them, the first delay cascades onto the rest of the day.
6. Color-code your blocks by type of work (deep work, communication, admin) — at a glance you can tell whether the day has a reasonable mix or is overloaded with one type of activity.

## The best tools

- **The calendar you already use** (Google Calendar, Outlook) — timeboxing doesn't require any special app, just add tasks as regular events.
- **Todoist or another task manager with calendar integration** — lets you drag a task straight from your to-do list into a calendar slot without retyping it by hand.
- **Sunsama or a similar planning tool** built specifically around the timeboxing principle — pulls tasks from multiple sources and forces you to schedule them into concrete blocks every day.
- **A timer (Pomodoro app)** as a complement inside individual blocks, if you need extra micro-structure within a single task.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** an estimated hour a day, thanks to less jumping between tasks and a clear structure instead of constantly deciding “what now.”
- **A realistic picture of your day:** a calendar full of blocks shows you first thing in the morning whether the day is overloaded, instead of finding out at night that half the plan didn't happen.
- **Less procrastination:** a task with a specific time window is psychologically much easier to start than an item on an anonymous list.
- **Protection for important work from meetings:** a block in your calendar looks just as “occupied” as a meeting — coworkers see it and are less likely to schedule something else into that spot.

## Pro tip

Schedule the hardest task of the day into the block when you demonstrably have the most energy — for most people, that's the morning. Timeboxing only solves when, not how much effort a task needs; combining both (a demanding task in an energetic block) is what separates a day that merely looks planned from one that actually works.
