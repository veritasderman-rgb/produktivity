An hour of googling SUMIFS syntax versus one plain-English sentence — that's no longer a fair fight. Excel and Google Sheets have dozens of functions, and their exact syntax, argument count, and condition order are hard to remember even for people who work with spreadsheets every day. But describing what you want to calculate in ordinary words is enough — AI builds the formula for you, complete with an explanation of why it works and what would happen if you changed part of it.

## A typical scenario

A team lead needs to sum revenue for just the Prague branch in a report, but with hundreds of rows and half-built filters, they lose track of how to combine SUMIFS with two conditions. Without this tip: fifteen minutes searching a forum and copying someone else's formula that half-fits their columns, because the names and order are different. With this tip, they write AI one sentence with the actual column names and get back a working formula built exactly for their spreadsheet — plus an explanation of why that particular combination of functions does what they need.

## How to do it

1. Describe what you want to calculate in plain, specific terms — including exact column names or letters, e.g., “column B is revenue, column A is branch.”
2. Say which program you're working in — Excel or Google Sheets; the syntax differs in places.
3. Ask for an explanation too: “Explain what the formula does, so I can write it myself next time.” It helps you gradually learn formulas instead of just copying them.
4. Paste the formula into the cell and check the result against a row you can calculate by hand — it's the fastest way to catch a formula that's computing something other than what you wanted.
5. When a formula doesn't work, paste the exact error message (#N/A, #VALUE!) along with the cell it appears in — AI will use that to diagnose and fix it.
6. For more complex workbooks, have the formula commented directly in the cell or an adjacent column, so a month from now you know why it's there and what it calculates.

## The best tools

- **ChatGPT / Claude / Gemini** — describe the task in plain language, get a formula plus an explanation, works the same for Excel and Sheets.
- **Microsoft Copilot in Excel** — if you have Microsoft 365, you can ask right inside the spreadsheet and Copilot will drop the formula into the right place itself.
- **The built-in function help in the program itself** — for a quick check of what arguments a function expects, when you're not sure what AI gave you.
- **A forum or community around Excel/Sheets** — as a second safety net for truly unusual requests where an experienced person's eye would help.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** fifteen minutes of searching and tweaking a formula shrinks to a minute of describing and checking.
- **Sanity:** no more frustration over forgotten syntax, missing semicolons, and wrongly calculated ranges.
- **Fewer errors:** a finished formula with an explanation is easier to verify than one copied from elsewhere without understanding why it actually works.
- **Extra knowledge:** you gradually learn, because AI explains the task instead of just solving it — next time you'll build a similar formula yourself.

## Pro tip

The same approach works just as well for debugging existing formulas — paste a broken formula with the question “why does it return #N/A?” and add a sample of the data it fails on. If the spreadsheet contains sensitive company numbers, replace the actual values with sample ones that keep the same structure — deriving a formula usually only requires knowing how the data is arranged, not its real content.
