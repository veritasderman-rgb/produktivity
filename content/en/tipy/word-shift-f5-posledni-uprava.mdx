You open a thesis in progress or a fifty-page report, and Word drops you on page one. What follows is a ritual: scroll, try to remember where you actually left off, hunt for the last unfinished sentence. Yet Word remembers the location of your last edit — and the <kbd>Shift</kbd> + <kbd>F5</kbd> shortcut jumps there instantly. It also works mid-session: if you hop over to fix something on page 3 and want to get back to page 40 where you were writing, the same shortcut takes you back.

## How to do it

1. In an open document, press <kbd>Shift</kbd> + <kbd>F5</kbd>. The cursor jumps to wherever you last changed something.
2. Pressing it repeatedly cycles through the last several edit locations — Word remembers more than just one, so you can “hop” between two or three passages you're working on in parallel.
3. The shortcut also works right after opening a saved document: press it as the very first thing and you're back where you left off last time. Newer Word versions even offer a “Welcome back” bubble with a jump to your last spot — but Shift+F5 works reliably even where that bubble doesn't appear.
4. On a laptop where the function keys control volume and brightness, you may need to add the <kbd>Fn</kbd> key: <kbd>Fn</kbd> + <kbd>Shift</kbd> + <kbd>F5</kbd>.
5. The alternative shortcut <kbd>Ctrl</kbd> + <kbd>Alt</kbd> + <kbd>Z</kbd> does the same thing — useful where Shift+F5 conflicts with something else or where F5 is awkward to reach.

It's worth knowing exactly what the shortcut tracks: places where you edited text, not places you merely clicked or scrolled to. So if you're only browsing a document, the history doesn't fill up — and the jump always takes you back to where real work last happened.

## A typical scenario

Kate is writing her thesis. Every evening she opens a document over sixty pages long, and before she finds the paragraph where she stopped yesterday, she spends a minute scrolling and reading to reorient herself — and sometimes gets sidetracked along the way by something that catches her eye. With Shift+F5, she opens the file, presses the shortcut, and writes — zero scrolling, zero searching.

It helps even more while editing: she fixes a typo in the intro that her advisor flagged, and instead of paging all the way back to the end of chapter 4, she just presses Shift+F5 twice and is back where she was editing before.

## What you get out of it

A few dozen seconds every time you open a longer document, and above all, no falling out of context — you return exactly to the thought you were in the middle of, not to the title page. For documents you switch between several times a day, it adds up to minutes; for a two-hundred-page paper, it's the difference between “writing right away” and “first, five minutes of searching.”

## Pro tip

To make jumping between chapters even faster, combine Shift+F5 with [heading styles and the navigation pane](/tipy/word-styly-nadpisu): use navigation to jump into a chapter, and Shift+F5 to return to your last edit.
