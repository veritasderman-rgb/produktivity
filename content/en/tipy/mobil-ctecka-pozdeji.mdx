Reading every interesting article the moment you see it means letting yourself be distracted on command. A coworker sends a link, you spot an eye-catching headline while scrolling, a friend recommends a long interview — and if you open it and start reading right away, you've just let someone else's content decide what you focus on next. A read-it-later app flips the order: you decide when, not the content.

## A typical scenario

Jana is a student who gets ten to fifteen “must read” article links a day from classmates and social media between classes. Without a system, that meant a browser with twenty open tabs, half of which she never finished reading anyway, plus the nagging feeling that something was always hanging over her. Focusing on a lecture or her own work kept getting interrupted by the temptation to “just glance at one of those articles.”

Today she has a reader app like Instapaper on her phone. An interesting article gets saved with two taps and she moves on — no reading now, no extra open tab. In the evening, when she has time and quiet, she opens the app and works through the saved articles in a batch. By then she doesn't actually want to read most of them anymore, because the moment of impulse has passed — and that's exactly the intended effect, not a loss. A week later, only what genuinely caught her attention enough to read a second time is still sitting in the app — the rest quietly disappeared, without ever breaking her focus during the day.

## How to do it

1. Pick a tool: a dedicated reader like Instapaper or Pocket, or just use the reading list built into your browser (Safari Reading List, Chrome Reading List).
2. Install the share extension or shortcut so saving works from any app — browser, email, social media.
3. Interesting article → Share button → Save to reader. It takes two seconds and no reading happens in that moment — just setting it aside for later.
4. Set a regular batch-reading time — evening after work, on the weekend, on the train. Open the reader and work through what you collected during the day.
5. Don't be afraid to delete unread items. You'll end up not wanting to read half of what you saved, and that's fine — the point of the system is to separate the moment of interest from the actual need to read, not to force you to finish everything.
6. Most readers support offline mode too — read saved articles without a signal, say on the subway or a flight.
7. If your unread list grows into the dozens, don't be afraid of a one-time cleanup — delete anything older than a month. Whatever truly mattered, you'll find again.

## The best tools

- **Instapaper** — simple, fast, focused purely on distraction-free reading, a solid default choice.
- **Pocket** — a popular alternative with similar save-and-read-offline features, connected to a range of browsers and apps.
- **Your browser's reading list (Safari, Chrome)** — the built-in and simplest option if you don't want to install another app.
- **Readwise Reader** — for power readers, it can also highlight passages and save notes from them for later.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** roughly 15–20 minutes a day that would otherwise go to impulsive reading in the middle of other work.
- **Focus:** fewer open tabs and fewer interruptions mean deeper work on whatever you're actually doing.
- **Peace of mind:** no “I have to read this right now” feeling — the article isn't going anywhere, it's waiting in the reader.
- **Better reading quality:** whatever you save for the evening gets read with genuine attention, not squeezed between two other tasks.
