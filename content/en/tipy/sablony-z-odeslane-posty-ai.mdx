Most people don't have reply templates, for one of two reasons: either they never got around to writing any, or they downloaded ready-made ones from the internet and stopped using them within a week because they sounded like someone else. Yet the best source of templates has been sitting there for years, and you've never touched it. **Your Sent folder holds hundreds of your own replies — and buried in them are eight or ten situations you deal with over and over, writing each one from scratch every time.** All you have to do is find them and pull them out.

## A typical scenario

Julia runs a small agency and sends around thirty emails a day. It feels to her like every one is unique. When she has her sent mail from the last twelve months analyzed, a different picture emerges: 63% of her replies fall into nine recurring types. Confirming a meeting time. Politely declining an offer. Requesting missing materials from a client. Chasing an unpaid invoice. Referring someone to a colleague. Responding to an inquiry that doesn't fit the budget. Rescheduling. Thanking someone after a meeting. Answering “how much does this cost.”

Nine templates cover six replies out of ten. Julia didn't get them from the internet — they came from her own phrasing, including the fact that she signs off with “Thanks, have a good one” and always offers an alternative time when declining something. Writing an email went from minutes down to seconds, and more importantly, the blank-page moment disappeared: now she edits a finished draft instead of staring at an empty window.

## How to do it

1. **Connect your mail to AI through a connector.** Claude can link to external services through an open standard called MCP — Gmail among them. Mail is sensitive by definition: it contains personal data, business terms, and things other people wrote to you. Do this **only on a paid or business account with contractual data protection, never in a free chat.** You grant the permission, and you can revoke it anytime.
2. **Ask for analysis first, not writing.** The first task isn't “write me some templates” — it's “go through my sent mail from the last twelve months and find the eight to ten most common types of reply; for each one, note how often it occurs and what those emails have in common.” You want to see the map first, the text second.
3. **Ask for templates in your own style.** This is the whole point of the exercise. State explicitly: base these on my actual phrasing, keep my greeting, sign-off, tone of formality, and sentence length; don't add phrases I've never used. Fields to fill in should be in square brackets — name, date, amount.
4. **Review and trim.** Read each template and cut ruthlessly. A template longer than two paragraphs won't actually get used in practice. And check that no specific data survived from the original emails — client names, prices, internal information.
5. **Store them where you write.** In your mail client as templates ([see templates in Gmail](/tipy/gmail-sablony)), or in a text expander that triggers with a shortcut in any window ([text replacement](/tipy/mac-text-replacement)). A template you have to hunt for isn't a template.
6. **Repeat after a quarter.** Things change. A fresh analysis will show which templates you never used (retire them) and what new type of email has shown up in the meantime.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** for someone writing twenty to thirty emails a day, conservatively half an hour a day. Not because you type faster, but because the blank-page moment is gone.
- **Consistency:** the same situation gets the same quality of reply even on a Thursday evening when you have no energy left for phrasing.
- **Quality:** a template built from your best version of an email beats improvising in a rush.
- **Self-insight:** the analysis itself is valuable even without the templates. You see, in black and white, what's actually eating your day.

## Pro tip

Have it separately list out the emails that **took you the longest to write** — the long replies to complicated situations (complaints, explaining a mistake, negotiating a price). These are the ones least likely to become templates, because “every one is different” — and yet they have the sturdiest skeleton: acknowledge the problem, state the facts, propose a solution, give a deadline. A template doesn't have to be finished text; it can be an outline. And hold the rule that applies everywhere on this site: **AI proposes, the person approves.** A tool with mail access is allowed to read and draft — only you send.
