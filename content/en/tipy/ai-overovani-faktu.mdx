The biggest risk with AI isn't that it can't do something. It's that it can't reliably say “I don't know” — and it states an error with exactly the same confidence as the truth, smoothly and without hesitation. Language models occasionally hallucinate: they invent a citation, mix up a section of law, or cite a number that appears nowhere in the source material, and the whole answer still sounds completely credible. The more the output matters, the more important it is to verify it before sending it on.

## A typical scenario

An assistant has AI summarize an amendment to a law and gets back a reference to a specific section that sounds credible and fits the context. Without verification: the section cited turns out to be numbered differently or was repealed long ago, and the mistake only surfaces later, damaging the credibility of the whole document, not just that one line. With a habit of verifying, she looks up the reference in the original text of the law before sending it on — and catches the mistake before it ever leaves her screen, when it still only costs a few minutes to fix.

## How to do it

1. Recognize the high-risk categories — specific numbers, citations, legal references, people's names, prices. This is exactly where AI hallucinates most often, with complete confidence.
2. For important output, always ask for sources: “Where does that number or claim come from?” — and actually open and check the sources.
3. Calibrate how much you verify based on the stakes — an internal brainstorm can tolerate a small inaccuracy, an email to a client or a legal document can't.
4. For numeric output, ask to see the calculation, not just the result — it's easier to spot where a mistake crept in.
5. Treat hedging language as a warning sign — if AI writes “approximately” or “usually,” that's an estimate, not a verified fact.
6. For facts you use repeatedly (price lists, contacts, internal figures), maintain one verified source and always point AI to it, rather than relying on its general memory.

## The best tools

- **Checking the source directly** (the law, an official document, a website) — the most reliable option, and free, it just takes time.
- **AI with web access and citations** (ChatGPT with browsing, Gemini, Perplexity) — makes it easier to track down a current source for a claim.
- **A second round in the same or a different conversation for cross-checking** — “check this claim and tell me if it holds up.”
- **A colleague or an expert as a last line of defense** — for legal, financial, and health-related topics, human review is irreplaceable.
- **Your own verified materials uploaded to AI** — if AI is only working from your document, the risk of hallucination drops, because it doesn't have to guess at anything.

## What you get out of it

- **Reputation:** a mistake caught by you, not by a client or leadership, is cheap; a mistake caught out in the open is expensive and hard to walk back.
- **Peace of mind:** you know what you can trust from AI right away and what always needs checking — less uncertainty working with its output.
- **Better decisions:** decisions built on verified numbers are more reliable than ones built on confident-sounding but incorrect text.
- **A habit:** over time you learn which topics AI tends to get wrong more often, and you tighten your checking there automatically, without having to think it through every time.

## Pro tip

A good habit is to ask directly: “Tell me what you're not sure about.” Good models will admit their level of uncertainty — but usually only when asked. Without the question, they'll answer with the same confidence whether it's a verified fact or a guess, and the difference between the two stays hidden.
