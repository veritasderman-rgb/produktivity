Scheduling a meeting for five people can turn into a two-day job — "Tuesday works for me," "I can only do mornings," "I'm traveling then." Yet if you're at a company or school with shared calendars (Google Workspace or Microsoft 365), that whole email survey is unnecessary: your calendar can lay every invitee's schedule side by side and you simply see the open slot. Most people never open this feature because it's tucked away inside an in-progress event.

## How to do it

**Google Calendar:**

1. Create an event and add guests.
2. Switch to the **Find a time** tab — a day view appears with every attendee's calendar in columns side by side. A blank stretch across all the columns = a time when everyone's free; click it and the event moves there.
3. Even faster is the **Suggested times** link below the guest list: Google lists the nearest conflict-free slots for you automatically.

**Outlook:**

1. In the meeting window, open the **Scheduling Assistant**.
2. You'll see a timeline showing every invitee's availability; drag the vertical bar to pick a window where nobody has anything booked. The assistant even distinguishes "tentative" from "out of office," so you can tell a hard conflict from a meeting the person could probably skip. You can add a conference room too — it has its own calendar just like a person.

The condition in both cases: attendees are in the same organization, or at least share free/busy information with you. Outside your company, the feature sees nothing — that's where a [booking page](/tipy/kalendar-rezervacni-stranka) comes in.

## A typical scenario

A deputy principal is scheduling a subject-committee meeting: six teachers, each with a different schedule. It used to mean a shared spreadsheet of who's free when, filled in over a week. Now she creates the event, adds her colleagues, opens Find a Time — and within a minute sees that the only slot everyone shares is Thursday at 7am. The invite goes out immediately, with zero back-and-forth emails.

## What you get out of it

Finding a time turns into a thirty-second task instead of an email round trip. And because you're picking from times when everyone is genuinely free, there's less last-minute rescheduling caused by a conflict nobody flagged. For recurring meetings with a larger group, it's the difference between "schedule a meeting" being a five-minute task and a week-long project.

## Pro tip

The feature is only as good as the calendars it reads. So it's worth agreeing on a team rule: if it's not on the calendar, it doesn't exist — including [blocks for focused work](/tipy/kalendar-bloky-prace). Anyone who doesn't block their deep-work time will happily have it filled by the scheduling assistant, because it looks free.
