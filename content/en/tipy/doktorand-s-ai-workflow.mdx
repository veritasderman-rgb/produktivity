Being a PhD student is a strange job: half researcher, half teacher, half administrator — and yes, that's three halves. Most of that work is mechanics that AI can now handle a good chunk of for you: working through the literature, processing papers, prepping exercises, grading tests. **What it can't handle is the part that matters: the original research idea, the responsibility for a grade — and the relationship with your students.** This tip is about freeing up your hands for those three things.

## A typical scenario

Mark is in his second year of a PhD. He teaches two sections, supervises term papers, and on top of that has his own research and an advisor who expects progress every month. His week has a shape: Monday, reading new papers in the field; Tuesday, prepping teaching materials; Wednesday, sections; Thursday, grading and emails to students; Friday — in theory — his own dissertation. In practice, what's left for it is Friday evening.

After putting an AI workflow in place, the week looks different. A scheduled deep-research routine hands him a review of new papers every Monday morning — what came out, what's relevant to his topic, with links. Section materials come together from his slides and two papers in an hour instead of an afternoon. He scans the tests, and AI proposes a score for each one against his answer key — he checks every proposal, corrects it, and signs off. The dissertation gets two full days.

## How to do it

1. **Schedule a research routine.** Set up deep research as a recurring task: "every Monday, go through what's new on topic X, summarize the relevant papers, and attach links." You still have to read everything yourself — you just no longer have to hunt for it.
2. **Process papers through your own source library.** Upload PDFs of papers into NotebookLM or a Project: summaries, comparisons of methodology, data extracted into a table. The same rules apply to your own dissertation as to [a bachelor's thesis with AI](/tipy/bakalarka-s-ai-poctivy-workflow) — scripts run the analysis, and you check every citation against the original.
3. **Build teaching prep from your own material.** Give AI your slides, your course notes, and two key papers: "prepare a 90-minute exercise session — five problems from easy to hard, with a solution and the most common student mistake for each." Turn the artifact into a handout, and the outline into [a presentation](/tipy/z-dokumentu-prezentace-ai).
4. **Grading tests: AI proposes, you sign off.** Run scanned tests — even handwritten ones, AI can read the handwriting — against your answer key: a proposed score plus reasoning for each answer. Then comes the ironclad rule: **you review every proposal, and you assign the grade.** A grade is a decision with consequences for a real person — AI can be a fast first reader, never the final word. And watch your data: student names and results are personal data — anonymize (numbers instead of names), use an institutional account with contractual data protection, and follow your university's AI policy.
5. **Turn email and admin into a routine.** Answers to repeat student questions, feedback for office hours, progress reports to your advisor — [templates built from your own sent mail](/tipy/sablony-z-odeslane-posty-ai) and a routine that drafts them. You still send them yourself.
6. **Disclose what needs disclosing.** Universities and journals have rules for AI use in research and teaching — and those rules are being written right now. Find out what they are and follow them; transparency is cheaper than getting caught out.

## The best tools

- **Claude with scheduled tasks** — the Monday research routine, email drafts, prep material on a schedule.
- **[NotebookLM](https://notebooklm.google.com)** — a library of papers on your topic: it answers only from the sources you upload and cites where it got each answer.
- **Claude Cowork** — a folder of scanned tests → proposed scores against your answer key, a results table for a final check.
- **Python scripts** — analysis for your dissertation: exact, reproducible, defensible.

## What you get out of it

- **Time for research:** two days a week that mechanics used to swallow — that's the difference between a four-year PhD and a six-year one.
- **Consistent grading:** an answer key applied by machine the same way to the first test and the fiftieth — you're policing fairness, not your own attention at eleven at night.
- **Better teaching:** prep no longer eats your afternoon, so you walk into a section with five good problems instead of two rushed ones.

## Pro tip

The remaining ten percent of a PhD student's job that AI can't handle is also the most important part: your own research question, the responsibility for a grade — and beer with students after a lecture. Leave that third one to humanity for now; we still hold a comfortable lead there. More seriously: mentoring and the relationship with your students are what they'll remember you for. AI frees up time for that — don't let it replace it.
