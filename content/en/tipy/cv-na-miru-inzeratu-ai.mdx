Most people have one résumé and send it everywhere. But the recruiter is reading the job listing, not your résumé — and scanning it for words they wrote into the listing themselves. If you spent two years doing “customer support” and the role is looking for someone in “client onboarding,” that's nearly the same thing, just in different language. **AI can reframe your actual experience into the vocabulary of a specific role and reorder it so the relevant parts sit in the top third of the page.** It must not invent anything in the process — and that rule is what the whole approach stands or falls on.

## A typical scenario

Mark is finishing his master's degree and applying for a junior marketing role. He has one generic résumé: part-time jobs, a semester project, six months running a student club, two years of retail sales at an electronics store. He sent it to twelve postings and got two replies.

Then he tries a different approach. For one specific listing, he uploads his résumé and the posting text and asks for an analysis: what the listing asks for, which of that his résumé actually covers, and what's missing. It turns out “running a student club” covers exactly three things the listing wants — event planning, sponsor communication, and social media management — except in his résumé it was one line with no detail. The retail job, meanwhile, means customer-facing work and product knowledge, which the listing mentions twice. The rewritten version has the same facts, different order, different vocabulary. Out of four more applications, he got two interviews. It took him twenty-five minutes instead of an hour and a half.

## How to do it

1. **Upload both at once: your résumé and the full listing text.** Don't rewrite the listing into a few bullet points — copy the whole thing, including the passages about company culture and “what you'll get here.” That's usually where the vocabulary the company uses about itself lives.
2. **Ask for an analysis first, not a finished résumé.** The brief: what the role requires, which of those requirements your résumé covers and how specifically, and which it doesn't cover at all. That second list is just as valuable as the first — you know what to prepare for in an interview and what's not worth faking.
3. **Set a hard rule before you let it write anything.** State explicitly in the prompt: **don't invent anything. You may rephrase and reorder what's already in my résumé. You may not add any skill, tool, number, or experience that isn't there. If something is missing, list it separately, but don't put it in the résumé.** Without this sentence, the model will add “experience with analytics tools” and you'll find out at the interview, where it shows within two questions. A made-up line on a résumé isn't a clever move — it's the fastest way to make an interviewer stop trusting the rest of it too.
4. **Have it reorder, not just reword.** The most valuable change is often not the phrasing but what's at the top. Ask for relevant experience to lead and less relevant experience to shrink to one line. People read the top third carefully and skim the rest.
5. **Write the cover letter as step three, not step one.** Only once the résumé fits, ask for a letter of at most half a page, built around three specific things from the listing, each one saying how you cover it. Then **rewrite it in your own voice.** A letter that reads like generated text is instantly obvious to recruiters.
6. **Check the facts line by line.** Go through the result and, for every sentence, ask yourself: is this true, and could I expand on it in an interview for two minutes? Cut what doesn't pass. The site's core rule applies here too: **AI proposes, the person approves** — and here you're approving claims about yourself.

- **A text editor of your choice** — do the layout in your own document; take text from AI, not formatting.
- **Notes with your own examples** — keep collecting specific numbers and results from your work over time. You can only reframe what you've actually written down.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** an hour per application drops to roughly a third of that. That's the difference between “I'll send three careful applications” and “I'll send ten.”
- **Relevance:** the same facts described in the role's vocabulary get past the first screen, where keyword matches often decide.
- **Confidence:** you know your gaps in advance, so you're not caught off guard by a question about something you can't do.
- **Consistency:** the résumé and cover letter come from the same analysis and don't contradict each other.

## Pro tip

Once your résumé is done, have it generate **fifteen likely interview questions** from the listing — ten technical and five about the gaps in your résumé. Practice your answers out loud; try the three hardest ones as a [dry run with AI playing the other side](/tipy/nacvik-tezkeho-rozhovoru-ai). And watch your data: a résumé is a concentrate of personal information — address, phone, employer names, sometimes date of birth. Use a **paid or business account with contractual data protection, not a free chat**, and strip out anything you don't need for the analysis beforehand. The analysis works just as well on a résumé with no contact details.
