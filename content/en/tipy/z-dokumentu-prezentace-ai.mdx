The worst way to build a presentation is opening PowerPoint and starting to type on slide one. You instantly get tangled up in fonts, bullets, and alignment, while the hard question — what am I actually trying to say, and in what order — stays unanswered. Yet the source material usually already exists: a report, a proposal, a strategy doc, meeting notes. **Nail the structure first, then worry about the visuals — doing it the other way around is where most of the time you lose on presentations goes.**

## A typical scenario

Product manager Jane has a sixteen-page report on pilot results and two days to present it to leadership in twenty minutes. Her usual approach: open a template one evening, spend three hours copying paragraphs onto slides, end up with twenty-eight slides full of text nobody can actually look at, and skip half of them anyway.

This time she uploads the report and asks for a structure first: “Leadership audience, twenty minutes, the decision is whether to expand the pilot. One slide, one idea. Propose ten to twelve slides, each with a headline stated as a claim and three bullet points.” Two minutes later she has an outline, and in it she can see that two numbers from the report don't actually support the conclusion, and there's no slide on cost at all. She reorders things, has a clickable prototype built as an artifact, and clicks through it as if she were presenting. Only then does she open the company template — and she knows exactly what belongs on each slide. Twenty minutes to a usable first draft, an hour for final polish.

## How to do it

1. **Upload the source and give it context, not a task.** “Turn this into a presentation” produces a document retold as slides. Say **who you're presenting to, how long you have, and what decision the audience needs to make.** Leadership wants the conclusion up front, a team wants the process, a client wants the benefit — one report can become three different presentations.
2. **Ask for a structure, not text.** First, just an outline: each slide's headline and its purpose in one sentence. At this stage, reordering is easy; once slides are laid out, nobody moves anything around anymore. Hold to the rule **one slide = one idea**, and phrase headlines as claims (“The pilot cut complaints by a fifth”), not labels (“Results”).
3. **Compress paragraphs into bullets.** A paragraph from a report is not a bullet point. Set a limit — at most three or four bullets per slide, each under ten words — and move anything that doesn't fit into the speaker notes. An audience either reads or listens; it can't do both at once.
4. **Have it build a clickable prototype as an artifact.** This is where the biggest time savings live. Claude can build a functioning page right in the chat that you click through like a presentation — see [artifacts](/tipy/claude-artefakty-miniappky). You see the flow and the length before you've even opened PowerPoint, and when something's off, you fix it with one sentence instead of an hour of moving text boxes.
5. **Click through it out loud and on time.** You'll notice two things: where a transition between slides is missing, and that you have a quarter more slides than actually fit. Do your cutting here, not during the actual presentation.
6. **Only now open PowerPoint.** Lay the polished structure into the company template — and if you don't have a properly set-up template, start there; see [a PowerPoint master template](/tipy/powerpoint-predloha). With a finished template, laying it out is mechanical work that takes twenty minutes.

## The best tools

- **[Claude](https://claude.ai)** — structure proposals from an uploaded document, plus an artifact as a clickable prototype where you tune the flow before laying anything out.
- **PowerPoint and Google Slides** — where the final deliverable gets made; with a set template and a solid outline, this is the fastest part of the whole process.
- **[Canva](https://canva.com)** — when you don't have a company template and need a decent look without a designer.
- **Speaker notes** — the most underused feature of presentation tools; everything you cut from the slides belongs here.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** roughly two hours per presentation. The biggest saving isn't in the writing — it's in not rebuilding finished slides over and over.
- **Structure:** logical gaps in your argument show up in the outline, where they're fixed in a minute — not the day before the meeting.
- **Concision:** the one-idea-per-slide rule is much easier to hold to when someone other than the author is doing the compressing.
- **Peace of mind:** you go into layout knowing the structure holds. What's left is craft, not decision-making.

## Pro tip

Before you lay out anything, have the whole presentation summarized into **one sentence the audience should walk away with** — then check whether every slide adds something toward it. Slides that merely “add context” are usually the ones that eat your time. And watch what you feed into AI: internal numbers, proposals, and client data belong on a **paid or business account with contractual data protection**, not a freely available chat; anonymize what you can. Then check the numbers on your slides against the original document — **AI proposes, the person approves.**
