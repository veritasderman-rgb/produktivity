Most lost deals and stalled projects didn't end on “no.” They ended on silence. You wrote, the other side put it off for later, later never came — and you either forgot to follow up or felt awkward chasing it. A follow-up watchdog solves this mechanically: it tracks who you wrote to and who hasn't replied, and after a set delay, it hands you a ready-made, polite reminder.

## A typical scenario

Eva, a consultant, sends out five proposals in a week. Two get replies on their own, three go quiet. Eva doesn't set a reminder because “I'll remember,” and three weeks later only one of them comes to mind. She follows up late and with an apology. One proposal falls through the cracks entirely — the client picks someone who reached out on time.

With a watchdog in place, it looks like this: on the fifth day with no reply, a short message appears in the Drafts folder, following up on the original thread, in the same tone, with a summary of what it was about. Eva reads it, shelves it for one client, sends it for two others. It takes two minutes. Silence stopped being an accident and became a process.

## How to do it

1. **Decide what actually gets watched.** Not everything. Typically, sent emails containing a question or a proposal. The simplest approach is to tag such messages when you send them — or send them from a specific folder the automation monitors.
2. **Pick a delay based on the type.** A proposal: three to five business days. A routine question to a colleague: two days. A long negotiation: a week. One universal deadline usually fits nothing.
3. **Let the automation check for replies.** The condition is simple: is there a newer message from the other side in this thread? If yes, the watchdog stays quiet. If not, it starts preparing a reminder.
4. **Give AI a tone, not just a task.** A prompt like “write a short, polite reminder, follow up on the original message, restate what it's about in one sentence, offer help, don't push and don't guilt-trip.” Two to three sentences are enough — a longer reminder lowers the odds of a reply.
5. **Save it as a draft in the same thread.** Never as a sent message, and never as a new email — following up in the original thread reminds the other side of the context.
6. **Set up a daily two-minute pass.** Go through the drafts at once, say after lunch. Send some, hold some, delete some, because you know things the automation can't.
7. **Set a cap.** No more than two reminders per thread. A third one isn't a follow-up anymore — it's pestering.

## The best tools

- **Built-in email features** — Gmail can nudge you about older unanswered messages and bump a thread back into your inbox; Outlook offers snooze and reminders. The cheapest first step, and no data leaves your mailbox.
- **Gemini in Workspace and Copilot in Microsoft 365** — help you word the reminder right inside the environment your company has approved.
- **Zapier, Make, or n8n** — build out the whole chain: tracking the deadline, checking for a reply, preparing the draft; n8n if you want the data to stay on your own infrastructure.
- **A CRM (such as HubSpot)** — for sales conversations; tracking activity and reminders is one of its core features, and you get the history in one place.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** 15–20 minutes a day that would otherwise go into scrolling through sent mail and wondering “did they ever get back to me?”
- **Money:** a timely reminder is the cheapest sales tool you have. Deals rarely fall through because of price — they fall through because of silence.
- **Peace of mind:** you stop carrying a mental list of people who owe you a reply.
- **A better impression:** a short, to-the-point reminder after five days looks professional. A reproachful email after three weeks doesn't.

## Pro tip

Add a rule to the prompt that the reminder should always include an offer of a next step — say, two specific time slots, or a line like “just reply yes and I'll send the materials over.” Everyone will type “yes”; almost nobody replies to “I need to think it over.” And guard the boundary: the automation may prepare a draft, never send it — a single reminder that goes out automatically at the wrong moment can damage a relationship more than the silence itself. Run this routine through a company account with contractual data protections, and exclude threads with sensitive content from monitoring.
