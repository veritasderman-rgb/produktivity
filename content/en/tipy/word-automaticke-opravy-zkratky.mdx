Everyone knows AutoCorrect as the feature that turns “teh” into “the.” Few people know you can add your own pairs to that same dictionary — and turn it into a shortcut dictionary. Type three letters, and Word replaces them with a company's full name, a name with titles, or a sentence you type twenty times a day. And because the correction dictionary is shared across every Office app, the same shortcut works in Word, Excel, PowerPoint, and Outlook alike.

## How to do it

1. In Word, open **File → Options → Proofing → AutoCorrect Options**.
2. On the **AutoCorrect** tab, you'll find the “Replace text as you type” table. Type your shortcut into the left field (e.g., “vutb”), the full text into the right field (“Brno University of Technology”), and click **Add**.
3. Pick a shortcut that isn't an existing word — otherwise it'll expand in places you don't want it to. Combinations that don't occur in normal text work best: “xfa,” “ssro,” “addr1.”
4. From then on, just type the shortcut followed by a space or punctuation — the text replaces itself automatically. It works across Word, Excel, PowerPoint, and Outlook on the same computer.
5. On the rare occasion you don't want the replacement, press <kbd>Ctrl</kbd> + <kbd>Z</kbd> right after it expands — the original shortcut comes back.
6. A replacement can carry formatting too: select a finished, formatted piece of text in Word (say, a signature block with a bolded name), and only then open the AutoCorrect dialog — the right-hand field will already be pre-filled, and a **Formatted text** toggle will appear. The shortcut will then insert the text along with its look.
7. Delete a pair you no longer need at any time in the same dialog: find it in the list, select it, and click **Delete**.

## A typical scenario

An accountant writes documents daily full of phrases like “value added tax,” full client names like “Novák & Sons, Construction Company, LLC,” and her own office address. She types each of them many times a day, with the risk of typos in names that matter most in official documents.

After ten minutes of setup, she types “dph1,” “nov1,” and “addr1” — and documents fill up with correct, consistently identical names. A typo in a client's name simply can't happen, because she never types it by hand.

## What you get out of it

A phrase you type ten times a day takes one second instead of five — that's minutes a day of pure typing time. What matters more is consistency: institution names, titled names, and legal wording come out identically every single time and without typos, which saves complaints and awkward corrections on contracts and official documents.

## Pro tip

This trick only works inside Office apps. If you want shortcuts to expand absolutely everywhere in the system, macOS has built-in [text replacement](/tipy/mac-text-replacement); on Windows, PowerToys serves a similar role. The Office version does have one advantage, though — your pairs travel with your Microsoft account between computers.
