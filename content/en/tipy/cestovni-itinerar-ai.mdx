Trip planning is fun for about twenty minutes. Then comes fifteen open tabs, three blogs with contradictory advice, and a map where you can't quite picture how far apart those points actually are — and after two hours you have a list of places, but still no plan. **AI handles this part in a few minutes: it builds a day-by-day skeleton where the transfers make geographic sense.** That skeleton isn't a timetable and shouldn't be mistaken for one, but it's exactly the part of the work nobody enjoys doing.

## A typical scenario

Sarah is a student heading to Lisbon for five days in September with a classmate, with a spending budget for local expenses. She wants to see the city, not check boxes: one museum and an hour in a café over three sights in a day. She'd already planned two evenings herself; the rest was up in the air.

She enters her dates, lodging in Alfama, a pace of “relaxed, at most two bigger things a day,” interests (architecture, food, views), and her budget. Two minutes later she has five days broken into half-day blocks, each transfer with an estimated time and mode of travel, and two food recommendations per day at her price level. The first version crams day three with a trip to Sintra plus half of Belém, so she writes “day three is too packed, cut it in half and move Belém to day five.” The second version fits. Thursday she spent verifying opening hours and reservations — the only hour she actually put into that part.

## How to do it

1. **Give it five things, not one sentence.** “Plan my Lisbon trip” returns the same list you'd find in any article. Write: dates and number of days, who's traveling (two adults, or two kids under ten), a daily budget, pace, and interests. Add where you're staying — without it, the plan will send you across town every single day.
2. **Ask for a specific output format.** You want it day by day, split into morning, afternoon, and evening, each block with an estimated transfer time and mode of travel. And explicitly ask for one optional extra item per day, in case something goes faster than expected.
3. **Iterate — don't start over.** The first version is raw material. “Day two is too packed, cut it back,” “add more food, fewer museums,” “drop anything with an entry fee over a certain amount” — each sentence moves the plan forward. A conversation is faster than trying to nail the perfect brief on the first try; see [talk to AI, don't shoot blind](/tipy/ai-iterace).
4. **Have it draft a plan B for bad weather.** For each outdoor day, ask for an indoor alternative in the same neighborhood. This is the part you'd never plan yourself, and then you're standing under an awning in the rain, scrolling your phone for options.
5. **Verify opening hours, prices, and reservations at the source.** This is the one mandatory step in the whole process. Language models state inaccuracies with the same confidence as facts, and opening hours are among the details that change most often. Check the actual websites of the places, and make reservations yourself. **AI proposes, the person verifies and books** — [verifying outputs](/tipy/ai-overovani-faktu) has very tangible consequences for travel, since a closed museum eats half your day.
6. **Save the plan offline.** Have the result rewritten into a simple page or note you can access on your phone even without signal. Abroad, that's the difference between a usable plan and a nice file you can't reach.

## The best tools

- **[Claude](https://claude.ai)** — can turn the plan directly into an artifact: a clickable page with each day laid out that you open on your phone and share with travel companions via a link.
- **AI search tools with citations** — for the verification pass, where you can see the source behind every claim; see [research with verifiable sources](/tipy/ai-vyhledavani-citace).
- **Maps with custom layers** — drop the itinerary's points into your own map. Seeing them together instantly shows you which day has nonsensical transfers.
- **[Notion](https://notion.com)** — if you're traveling in a bigger group: one page with the plan, a packing checklist, and reservation links all in one place.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** a whole afternoon of searching shrinks to twenty minutes of input and an hour of verification.
- **Realism:** you set the pace. A plan “with two kids” looks nothing like a plan for “we want to see as much as possible” — and that distinction is exactly what generic travel articles can't offer.
- **Peace of mind:** a rainy-day backup and one extra option per day mean the trip doesn't fall apart when something doesn't work out.
- **Money:** a budget entered up front keeps the plan at the level you want, instead of finding out on the ground.

## Pro tip

Before you leave, have the itinerary generate two extra things: a **list of things to book in advance** (what needs a reservation and how far ahead) and a **packing list derived from the plan** — if the itinerary has a full-day hike and one dinner at a restaurant with a dress code, that should show up in your suitcase. And watch your data: a travel itinerary reveals when you're not home, who you're traveling with, and flight numbers. Use a **paid account with contractual data protection, not a free chat**, and never put scanned passports, boarding passes, or card numbers into the conversation at all.
