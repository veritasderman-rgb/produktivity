Your brain can't hold full attention for an hour straight. Stop asking it to. The Pomodoro technique splits studying into short, manageable blocks with regular breaks — and paradoxically, you get more done in a day than during one long, exhausting session where your attention drifts off after half an hour anyway. It's named after the tomato-shaped kitchen timer the technique's creator once used to measure it, but the principle is universal and works no matter what you use to keep time.

## A typical scenario

Student Filip plans a three-hour study block for Saturday afternoon. He's focused for the first hour, by the second he's checking his phone every five minutes, and by the third he's just mechanically flipping pages without actually remembering anything. At the end he feels like he “studied all afternoon,” but the results don't match.

With the Pomodoro technique, he splits the same amount of time into 25-minute blocks with short breaks in between. Each block is short enough that it's easy to get started — the big, intimidating task of “study the chapter” stops feeling so daunting when you know a break is coming in 25 minutes anyway.

## How to do it

1. Set a timer for 25 minutes and focus exclusively on one thing — no switching between subjects, no checking messages in between.
2. After 25 minutes, take a 5-minute break — stand up, walk around, stretch, but leave your phone alone. The point of the break is to rest your brain, not occupy it with a different kind of stimulation.
3. After four completed rounds (roughly two hours), treat yourself to a longer break, 20 to 30 minutes — only then move on to the next cycle.
4. If an unexpected thought or task interrupts a block, jot it down on paper and come back to it during the break — the goal is to keep one thing per block.
5. Before each block, briefly tell yourself exactly what you want to get done in those 25 minutes — a clearly defined goal for the block helps hold your attention better than a vague “I'll study.”

## The best tools

- **Your phone's built-in timer** — free and enough for the basic version of the technique, no special app required.
- **Forest** — a gamified app where a virtual tree grows during your block as long as you leave your phone alone, good for building the habit.
- **Focus To-Do** — combines a Pomodoro timer directly with a task list, so you can see how many blocks actually went into each task.
- **TomatoTimer** — a simple web-based timer with no sign-up, ideal if you don't want to install anything extra.

## What you get out of it

- **Better focus:** short blocks keep your attention at its peak for their entire length, unlike a long session where attention gradually drops off.
- **An easier start:** “25 minutes” sounds manageable even when you really don't feel like studying, unlike “I'll sit down for three hours.”
- **Measurable progress:** after every round you know exactly how many blocks you've put in today, which helps keep motivation up.
- **Less exhaustion:** regular breaks prevent mental burnout during a long study day.
- **A clear structure for the day:** you know in advance how many blocks the day should have, so studying doesn't dissolve into a vague “I'll study for as long as it takes.”

## Pro tip

For deep work where you need to really immerse yourself in a topic, try a longer rhythm of 50 minutes of work and 10 minutes of break. The rule stays the same no matter the block length: one single thing during the block, no screens during the break.
