Taking notes in a meeting means not being in the meeting. Whoever is writing isn't thinking — and still misses half of what happens anyway. Transcribing spoken words is something almost every tool can handle today, so manual note-taking has stopped making sense. But the real gain doesn't come from the transcript itself — it comes from what happens **automatically after** the meeting, when decisions and tasks fall out of the transcript and land directly in the task list.

## A typical scenario

A ninety-minute project meeting, seven people. Jana, the team lead, is jotting notes on a pad, so she only jumps into the discussion three times instead of ten. In the evening she writes up the notes from memory, can't quite recall two details, and phrases them vaguely. A week later comes the classic: "Wait, who was supposed to take that?" Nobody knows. The task never happened.

With an automatic pipeline, Jana's hands are free during the meeting. Within ten minutes of it ending, she has a structured draft: four decisions, six tasks with an owner and a deadline each, two open questions. The tasks are already sitting in the task list as drafts awaiting approval. Jana goes through them, rewords two, deletes one — and only then sends out the notes. The whole thing takes five minutes.

## How to do it

1. **Settle consent before the tech.** Recording and transcribing a meeting requires participants to know about it and agree to it. Say so out loud at the start, and for external meetings, follow up by email in advance too. No consent, no recording.
2. **Turn on transcription.** The simplest option is the built-in transcript in Teams or Google Meet. If you're meeting across platforms or in person, reach for a dedicated notetaker (Granola, Fireflies, tl;dv).
3. **Let AI extract the structure.** The transcript is raw material, not the output. Write the prompt as a template: decisions, tasks in a who-what-by-when format, open questions, date of the next step. And explicitly: "Don't add anything that wasn't said in the transcript."
4. **Connect it to the task list.** An automation platform (Zapier, Make, n8n) takes the task list and creates it in Todoist or Notion — always with a "pending approval" label or in its own project, so it doesn't mix in with the team's already-confirmed work.
5. **Add a review step.** Notes go out only after a human has read them. AI can miss sarcasm, misattribute a name, or turn "maybe we could" into a commitment.
6. **Save the notes with the project.** A shared folder or project page, not just an email. Notes that can't be found are as good as notes that don't exist.

## The best tools

- **Built-in transcription in Teams and Google Meet** — nothing to install, runs under your company account, which is the cleanest option from a data-protection standpoint.
- **Granola** — a notetaker that works alongside your own notes too; useful when you want to steer the notes, not just receive them.
- **Fireflies and tl;dv** — cross-platform notetakers with a searchable meeting archive and connections to other tools.
- **Zapier, Make, or n8n** — the link between the notes and the task list; pick n8n when everything needs to stay on your own infrastructure.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** 20–30 minutes after every meeting that used to go into writing up notes. At three meetings a week, that's an hour and a half.
- **Attention:** you can think and talk during the meeting instead of writing. That shows up in the quality of the discussion far more than any minutes saved.
- **Fewer disputes:** decisions and ownership are in black and white and come from what actually happened, not from one person's memory.
- **Traceability:** six months later, you can find out why you decided something the way you did.

## Pro tip

Have AI add one extra section to the notes: "What was discussed but nobody took on." That list is a goldmine — it's exactly where the things that come back as problems a month later are hiding. And watch your data: meetings with sensitive content (personnel matters, legal discussions, health information, trade secrets) belong only in tools your company has approved and that have data processing terms in place. If you're not sure, just turn off recording for that one meeting — the loss is smaller than the leak.
