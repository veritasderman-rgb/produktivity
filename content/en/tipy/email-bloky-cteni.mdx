Every “just a quick check of my email” costs more than it looks like — it's not the one minute spent reading the message, it's the trip back. Your brain needs time to re-tune itself to the work in progress that your inbox just pulled it away from, and that return trip takes noticeably longer than reading the email itself.

## A typical scenario

You're working on an important task, and your mail client keeps flashing a new notification in the corner of the screen. Without fixed email blocks, that means: interrupt your train of thought, read the message, maybe reply briefly, then painstakingly work your way back to where you left off in the task — and that might happen anywhere from twenty to sixty times a day. With three fixed blocks a day (say, morning, midday, and afternoon), email gets handled in batches, and the focused work in between stays uninterrupted for hours at a time.

## How to do it

1. Set three fixed time blocks for checking email, say at 9:00, 13:00, and 16:30, each about 20 to 30 minutes long — enough time to get through it, but not so much that the block stretches into the whole morning.
2. Outside those blocks, keep your mail client **closed** (not just minimized) and turn off notifications on both your computer and your phone — a muted notification with the app still open in the background still tempts you to click.
3. Within a block, work through messages in batches following a clear rule: anything you can handle in under two minutes, do right away; anything requiring longer work goes into your task system with a deadline; the rest gets archived or marked as read if it requires no action at all.
4. If you need to let coworkers know you're not on email constantly, mention it once in your signature or a short note to the team — so they know a reply will come during the next block, not instantly.
5. As a manager, be the first to adopt the blocks — if you keep replying to emails instantly in the evening and on weekends, your team unintentionally follows the same pace, no matter what the official rules say.

## The best tools

- **Built-in notification muting** in your mail client (Outlook, Gmail) or directly in the system — a basic step that costs nothing and does half the work.
- **A distraction blocker / focus timer** (like the Focus feature in Windows or macOS) — automatically silences notifications outside your defined blocks, so you don't have to remember to do it manually.
- **Rules and filters in your mail client** — automatically sorting incoming mail by sender or subject makes fast batch processing within a block easier.
- **A shared calendar or availability status** — lets coworkers know when you're on email and when you're heads-down, without you having to explain it repeatedly.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** roughly three-quarters of an hour a day saved from constant attention-switching and returning to work in progress.
- **Deeper focus:** long, uninterrupted stretches let you get into a state where work goes faster and with fewer mistakes.
- **Less end-of-day exhaustion:** constant attention-switching is mentally draining on its own, even when it doesn't look like demanding work from the outside.
- **A clearer inbox:** batch processing with a clear rule (handle / schedule / archive) keeps your inbox tidier than constant, unsystematic reading.

## Pro tip

Worried you'll miss something urgent between blocks? Genuinely urgent matters overwhelmingly come by phone or in person, not by email — email is, by its nature, a tool for asynchronous communication, not instant reaction. That worry usually resolves itself after the first week of trying the blocks.
