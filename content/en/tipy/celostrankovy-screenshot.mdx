You need to capture an entire web page — not just the part that fits on your monitor. A classic Print Screen only grabs the visible screen, so people take four screenshots in a row and paste them together in Paint, or install a sketchy extension. Yet Edge and Firefox have full-page screenshots built in, and Chrome can do it through a hidden command — all free, no installation required.

## How to do it

1. **Edge:** press <kbd>Ctrl</kbd> + <kbd>Shift</kbd> + <kbd>S</kbd> and choose **Capture full page**. Edge scrolls the page itself and offers the result for immediate annotation, copying, or saving.
2. **Firefox:** right-click the page, choose **Take Screenshot**, then the **Save full page** button (the shortcut Ctrl+Shift+S works too). You can copy the capture to the clipboard or download it as a PNG.
3. **Chrome:** press <kbd>F12</kbd> (developer tools will open — don't panic), then <kbd>Ctrl</kbd> + <kbd>Shift</kbd> + <kbd>P</kbd> and start typing "screenshot." Choose **Capture full size screenshot** — Chrome saves a full-page PNG to your downloads. Close the tools again with F12 when you're done.
4. The result is one long image at full resolution — suitable for documentation, complaint correspondence, or an archive.
5. For very long pages (infinite feeds, year-long statements), expect the file to run into the tens of megabytes — it's worth shrinking it before emailing, or sending a link instead.

## A typical scenario

A marketer is turning in a report to a client about a published article. Instead of three pasted-together crops, she sends one capture of the whole page — header, text, and footer, exactly as the page looked that day. The client sees the full context, and nobody has to piece anything together.

A second typical scene: a complaint. An online store confirmed your order at a certain price and with certain terms. A full-page screenshot of the confirmation page is evidence that captures everything, including the fine print at the bottom — not just a crop someone can claim is missing context. Six months from now, when the page looks completely different, you'll be glad you have a full copy of it.

## What you get out of it

Capturing a whole page is a five-second job instead of five minutes of stitching crops together. The captures are complete, in one file, and full quality — which matters every time a screenshot serves as backup or evidence.

## Pro tip

In Firefox, instead of the whole page, you can capture a specific element: after starting the capture, just hover over a paragraph, table, or image — Firefox frames it automatically and captures exactly to its edges. No shaky click-and-drag, no letters cropped in half.
