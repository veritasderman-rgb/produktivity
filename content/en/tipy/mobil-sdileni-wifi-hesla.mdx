"What's the Wi-Fi password here?" — and then someone hunts for a sticky note, reads out "capital B, lowercase r, four…," and the connection fails three times over a typo. Unnecessary. Both systems can hand over a password without anyone seeing or retyping it: iPhone shares passwords automatically between people you know, and Android turns any saved network into a QR code.

## How to do it

**iPhone → iPhone, iPad, or Mac:**

1. Your guest opens Settings → Wi-Fi on their device and taps your network.
2. On your unlocked iPhone, a **Share Password** card appears at the bottom — tap it and your guest's device connects. Nothing gets read aloud; the password transfers invisibly.
3. Conditions that occasionally make this "mysteriously" not work: both devices need Wi-Fi and Bluetooth turned on, and you need to be **in each other's contacts** (under the email tied to the other person's Apple account). You can view your own password under Settings → Wi-Fi → the (i) icon next to the network → Password.

**Android:**

1. Open Settings → Network & Internet (Connections on Samsung) → **Wi-Fi** and tap the network you're connected to.
2. Choose **Share** and confirm with your fingerprint or PIN. A **QR code** appears, and on most phones the password in readable form too.
3. Your guest scans the code with their camera — it works for guests with an iPhone too, since Wi-Fi QR codes are a universal standard. Once scanned, the device connects immediately.

Mixing the two: when an iPhone needs to hand a password to an Android device, automatic sharing won't work — use the displayed password instead, or the network's QR code from the Passwords app (iOS 18).

## A typical scenario

Visitors arrive in a conference room, and the meeting starts with three people retyping a twenty-character password off a sign on the wall. With a QR code on Android, you just pass the code around — everyone scans it and is connected within five seconds. Same thing at home with guests: instead of hunting for the sticky note from the router, you just show your screen.

And when a friend with an iPhone wants to connect at your cabin, you just unlock your phone, wait for the Share Password card, and tap it. She never sees the password — handy when it's the same one you use in three other places too.

## What you get out of it

Connecting guests stops being a ritual of reading passwords aloud and typos — minutes become a few seconds, every single time someone shows up. There's a security bonus too: you hand over the password without it ever appearing anywhere in readable form, so you don't have to change it after every visit or keep it written on the fridge.

## Pro tip

You can print your home network's QR code and hang it in the hallway or the conference room — visitors connect on their own, no questions asked. A code generated on Android is just a plain image: screenshot the sharing screen and you've got a permanent "ticket" to your Wi-Fi that both iPhone and Android can scan.
