A written chat with AI carries one hidden cost: before you can type a thought, you have to phrase it. That sounds harmless, but it's exactly the step where most people get stuck facing a blank page. Voice mode removes it — you talk the way you think, meaning messily, with tangents and corrections, and the other side works with that. In twenty minutes of walking you can get through more variations than in an hour of clicking through a chat.

**The core idea of this whole guide: voice mode isn't faster typing, it's a different kind of thinking.** It's not suited to producing text — it's suited to what writing does badly: exploring a problem from several angles, being pressed on things you skipped over, explaining something out loud and realizing in the process that you don't actually understand it. Anyone expecting a finished document from voice will be disappointed. Anyone expecting sorted-out thoughts will get them.

The guide has three parts. First, setup and conversation technique — how to talk so the conversation actually leads somewhere, and how to get notes out of it. Then specific uses: deciding and problem-solving out loud, studying and being quizzed, language practice. Finally, the limits you need to know before you hit them. The prompts here are written as sentences to say out loud — conversation openers and mid-conversation instructions you can read off the screen or memorize. One rule sits above all of it: **AI proposes, the human decides** — a voice conversation never produces a commitment on its own, only material to work from.

## A typical scenario

Lenka runs the product department and has to present a new strategy to leadership in two days. The idea sits in her head as a fog: she knows what bugs her about the current state, has a rough sense of where it should go, but hasn't put it together. She's sat down to it twice already — each time opening a blank document, writing three headings, rearranging them for twenty minutes, and closing it. The problem wasn't time; it was that writing requires a finished thought, and she didn't have one yet.

The second time, she tries something different. On the way to lunch and back, twenty-six minutes total, she starts voice mode and opens with: “I need to sort out my thinking for a presentation to leadership. I'm not going to talk in an organized way. Don't summarize what I say back to me — ask me about what I skipped.” What follows is a conversation in which she changes direction twice, contradicts herself once, and at one point realizes that what she'd considered her main argument can't be backed up with anything except a feeling.

That last moment is the whole payoff. The question “what exactly is the claim that the current process is slowing things down actually based on?” forces her to admit she doesn't know — and to decide either to leave it out of the presentation, or to check it against the data before evening. At the end of the walk she says: “Summarize where we landed, split it into what's decided, what's open, and what I need to verify.” Back at the office, she opens the summary and writes — not from scratch, but from an outline that's already sorted out in her head, because she spent the whole hour before saying it out loud.

## Phase 1: setup and the first thirty seconds

### Where to find voice mode

In the **ChatGPT**, **Gemini**, and **Claude** mobile apps, voice input sits right in the chat, usually behind a microphone or headphone icon. Keep two things apart that look similar: **dictation** (you talk, text appears, the reply comes back written) and **voice conversation** (you talk, you hear the reply). What this guide is about is the second one — in conversation mode you can walk around and never look at the screen.

The practical minimum: earbuds (for the mic, and so you can hear the reply outside), the app already downloaded, logged in before you leave, phone in your pocket. If you're trying voice mode for the first time, do it on a short walk with a topic that doesn't matter — the first conversation tends to feel awkward until you get used to being able to interrupt the other side.

### The first thirty seconds decide everything

The most common reason a voice conversation goes nowhere is that models default to summarizing, agreeing, and offering lists. That's tolerable in writing, but in your ear it's a disaster — you end up listening to bullet points you just said yourself a moment ago. The fix is simple: set the conversation mode in your opening sentences.

```text
We'll be talking for about twenty minutes, I'm walking while
we do it, and I won't be looking at the screen.

Please set yourself to this mode:
- answer briefly, three to four sentences at most
- don't summarize what I just said
- don't give me lists or bullet points, this is a conversation
- after each of your answers, ask me one question, not three
- if I say something that contradicts what I said earlier,
  point it out
- if I go quiet for a while, don't comment on it, just wait

Topic: [what I want to talk about]. Goal: by the end I want
[sorted-out arguments / a decision / an outline].
```

This is the single most important prompt in the whole guide. Without it, you'll spend twenty minutes listening to your own content read back to you. Pay special attention to the one-question line — models love firing off three at once, and you'll only remember the last one.

### Saving the setup so it doesn't have to repeat

If you use voice mode regularly, you don't have to dictate the setup every time. Put it into your custom instructions, or set up a project with persistent context where this style applies from the start. It's also worth adding a couple of sentences about what you work on, so the model doesn't explain the basics of your own field back to you.

## When voice beats writing

Voice mode isn't a better tool, it's a different tool. Here are five situations where it wins outright, followed by a list of where it loses.

### Brainstorming and mulling things over

When you're hunting for options, writing is a brake: phrasing each option also means evaluating it, so half the ideas never make it out at all. Say them out loud and you get three times as many, and the bad ones expose themselves just by how they sound.

### Learning and explaining

Explaining something out loud is the fastest test of whether you actually understand it. In writing you can hide behind phrasing; in speech you can't — either it flows, or you get stuck. And getting stuck is information.

### Working through a problem out loud

The classic programmer's “rubber duck”: you describe a problem to someone who doesn't know it, and halfway through describing it, the solution hits you. With AI, this method has the added benefit that it asks you questions back.

### Language practice

You learn a foreign language by speaking it, and the main obstacle is embarrassment. With AI that disappears — nobody laughs, you can repeat something as many times as you want, and the conversation is available at six in the morning or eleven at night.

### Hands-free situations

Walking, running, driving, cooking, cleaning, waiting. Time when you can't write but you can think — and with voice mode, think out loud with someone.

### And where voice loses

Don't use it for anything that needs to end up in **exact written form**: text that's going out, code, spreadsheets, long lists of numbers. Listening to numbers is painful and you'll retain a fraction of them. It's also a poor fit for working with a long source document — you can't flip back through something you heard ten minutes ago. And it doesn't fit anywhere someone can hear you: a voice conversation in an open space is public, including any names and numbers you say out loud in it.

The practical rule: **think with voice, produce with writing.** The conversation ends with a summary you carry over into written form, and that's where the actual output gets made.

### Where exactly it fits into your week

Before you start, it's worth going through your own week once and finding the spots where voice mode fits without taking time from anywhere. Write this prompt — don't dictate it — it's a one-time setup step.

```text
I'll describe my typical work week. Find the spots in it
where it's worth thinking out loud with AI instead of
writing.

My week: [regular blocks, meetings, commuting, moving
between places, recurring tasks, when I walk or drive].
The types of work that slow me down the most: [description].

Return a table: situation | how many minutes | what's worth
tackling with voice in it | what comes out of it (an outline,
a decision, notes).

Rules:
- only suggest windows that genuinely exist in my
  description, don't invent free time
- for each situation, say why it specifically suits voice
- at the end, list three things from my work where voice
  mode is a bad fit, and why
```

It typically turns up two or three windows you hadn't thought about — often the walk to lunch, moving between buildings, or fifteen minutes before picking up the kids. Read the last item carefully: the list of poor fits will save you from a few wasted walks.

## Phase 2: techniques for a conversation that actually goes somewhere

### Let yourself be pressed

The most valuable thing a voice conversation can do is apply the pressure of questions. Most people never experience it, though, because by default the model answers instead of asking. You have to explicitly ask for it — and then put up with how uncomfortable it is.

```text
I'm going to describe something that's on my mind, and I
want you to interrogate me about it.

Rules:
- don't advise, don't summarize, don't propose solutions
- ask one question at a time and wait for my answer
- ask mainly about what I SKIPPED — assumptions I never
  stated, and claims I have no basis for
- if I answer evasively, ask again in different words
- after ten questions, tell me what you understood from my
  answers, and let me correct it

Topic: [description]. Go.
```

It returns a series of questions that sting — and that's the point. Watch for two things: if the questions drift into generalities (“and what's your main goal?”), say out loud “ask more specifically, about things I actually said.” And if you catch yourself giving a long, evasive answer, that's a signal you've hit a spot where you don't actually have clarity.

### Think out loud, don't answer

Most people, in voice mode, answer briefly, like on a phone call. That's a shame — the payoff comes from talking at length and without much order. Open with “I'm going to talk for two minutes without interruption, just listen,” and then talk. The model has something to work from, and its questions turn specific instead of generic.

### Interrupt

Conversation modes handle being talked over just fine. Use that. The moment you hear an answer heading somewhere off-target, say “stop, that's not it, what I'm asking about is…” Politely sitting through a long answer is the fastest way to turn a twenty-minute walk into five minutes of you talking and fifteen minutes of listening.

### Steer the pace and depth

A handful of sentences worth keeping in reserve: “slow down, go back to that,” “skip that, I already know it,” “ask me the same thing again, but more specifically,” “push back on that now,” “sum up in one sentence where we are.” They work better than long instructions, because they land exactly when you need them.

### The closing summary: the most important step

A conversation that leaves no record is unrecognizable an hour later. So the last two minutes of the walk belong to a summary — and it has to be specified precisely, or you get a re-narration instead of a usable outline.

```text
Let's wrap up. Write me a text summary of our conversation,
I'll be reading it at my computer. Split it into five parts:

1. Where we landed — the main ideas, not a re-narration of
   the whole conversation
2. Decisions I made out loud during the conversation
   (only ones I actually stated)
3. What stayed open and what to do about it next
4. Arguments where I admitted I don't have anything backing
   them up — quote them verbatim
5. An outline it can all be organized into: headings, with
   two bullet points under each on what it should cover

Don't add anything that wasn't said in the conversation.
Where you're not sure whether something was my idea or
yours, say so.
```

It returns notes you can start writing straight from. Item 4 is the reason this is worth doing: a list of your own unsupported claims is the single most useful thing you can bring home from a walk. The “my idea / your idea” distinction belongs there because after twenty minutes of conversation you can't tell the difference yourself anymore — and it matters whether you're bringing your own thought into a meeting or a machine's suggestion.

### Continuity between walks

If you're working a topic over several days, keep the conversations in one thread or one project, so you don't have to start from zero every time. At the start of the next walk, this is enough: “Picking up from yesterday. Remind me in three sentences where we left off and what's still open, then let's continue.”

## Phase 3: deciding and problem-solving out loud

### When you don't know what's bothering you

The most common state isn't “I have two options and don't know which one.” It's “something about this doesn't sit right and I don't know what.” For that state, talking is incomparably better than writing, because it doesn't require you to be able to name the problem up front.

```text
Something's bothering me and I can't name it. I'm going to
describe it to you indirectly, through what's happening and
how it feels.

Your role: don't try to solve it right away. Help me name
it first.
- listen for what I keep coming back to
- if I return to the same thing twice, point it out
- after a while, offer me three different framings of what
  the real problem might be, and let me pick one or reject
  them
- only once we agree on a framing do we talk about solutions

Starting point: [describe the situation].
```

It returns candidate framings of the problem. Rejecting all three is a legitimate outcome — it often nudges you toward a fourth framing, your own, which was the goal all along.

### Deciding between options

Once you have options, you need something different: someone forcing you to state your criteria before you start defending your favorite one.

```text
I'm deciding between [option A] and [option B]. I don't
want a recommendation from you, I want you to walk me
through the decision.

Process, stick to it:
1. First, ask me for criteria — what should this decision
   be based on. Keep asking until I've named at least four,
   and make me rank them by importance.
2. Then go through option A against every criterion, ask
   me how it holds up on each, and don't rate it for me.
3. Same with option B.
4. Ask whether there's an option C I haven't considered.
5. Only at the very end: ask which option feels right to me
   after this conversation, and why. Don't comment on it.

I'm talking while walking, so keep your answers short.
```

It returns a structured conversation, at the end of which you usually know how to decide — not because the model told you, but because you had to state your criteria out loud. Step 4 pays off more often than you'd expect. And it still holds: **the human decides.** The model doesn't know half the context in your head, and it never heard the other half, because you never told it.

### Rehearsing a hard conversation

Voice mode is the one place you can try out an uncomfortable conversation out loud, with real pauses and real pressure. A written simulation can't do this, because it gives you time to phrase things — time you don't have in the real conversation.

```text
Play the other side of the conversation. You are [role, e.g.
a client asking for a discount / a colleague I need to tell
about an unwelcome decision]. I am [my role].

Context you know: [the situation, the history of the
relationship, what's at stake].
Your position: [what you're pushing for].
Your style: [matter-of-fact and firm / emotional / evasive].

Rules of the game:
- stay in character until I say “stop”
- don't be accommodating, push back on me like a real
  counterpart would
- react to what I actually say, don't assume anything I
  didn't say
- if I use a weak argument, use it against me

Once we're done, step out of the role and tell me: where I
was persuasive, where I gave ground unnecessarily, and two
sentences I should have ready for the real conversation.
```

It returns a realistic simulation plus a breakdown. Run it twice: the first time you focus on what you want to say, the second time on how you react. Related to the tip [rehearsing a hard conversation](/tipy/nacvik-tezkeho-rozhovoru-ai).

## Phase 4: studying and being quizzed by voice

### Explain it out loud and see where the gap is

The oldest study technique in new clothing: what you can't explain in your own words, you don't actually know. Voice mode is ideal for it, because you're explaining to someone who immediately asks about whatever you skipped.

```text
I'm going to explain the topic of [topic] out loud, as if
teaching it to you. Play a student who knows nothing about
it but is sharp and attentive.

What to do:
- if I use a term I haven't explained yet, ask what it means
- if I make a jump in my reasoning, ask how I got from
  point A to point B
- if I say something imprecise, don't correct me right
  away — ask a question that lets me notice the imprecision
  myself
- ask one question at a time

Once I'm done, tell me: which three parts of my explanation
were unclear, and what I skipped entirely.
```

It returns an experience that leaves you with a precise list of your own gaps. The method is merciless — expect the first pass at exam material to usually end with the discovery that you understand about half of it. Follows on from the tip [“explain it to me like…”](/tipy/ai-vysvetleni-latky).

### Oral quizzing while walking

Before an oral exam or a defense, voice has a clear edge over writing: you're training exactly the skill you'll need — speaking coherently under pressure.

```text
Quiz me on [subject / topic area]. You have twenty minutes,
I'm walking while we do it.

How to run it:
- ask like an examiner, not a quiz app: open-ended questions
  that need a coherent answer, not a one-word one
- after my answer, ask one follow-up question that goes a
  level deeper
- if I get it wrong, don't give the correct answer right
  away — steer me toward it with a question
- if I get it right but superficially, ask for an example
  or an exception

At the end, tell me: what I know well, what I only know
superficially, and what I still need to study. Be honest,
don't go easy on me.
```

It returns twenty minutes that pay off like an hour of reading. Apply the usual caution to any facts the model states while quizzing you — models make up dates, names, and definitions, so verify anything new against a textbook; see [verifying facts with AI](/tipy/ai-overovani-faktu).

## Phase 5: language practice

This is the one use case where voice mode has no real competition among commonly available tools: unlimited conversation in a foreign language, anytime, with no embarrassment and no need to schedule a session.

### Setting up the conversation so it actually pushes you forward

Without instructions, a voice conversation in a foreign language degenerates into one of two things: the model talks too complicated and you don't understand, or it talks too simple and you learn nothing. Set the level and the correction style right at the start.

```text
From now on we only talk in [language]. My level is roughly
[A2 / B1 / B2], I understand better than I speak.

Conversation setup:
- speak a bit slower than a native speaker and use
  vocabulary about one level above mine
- if I use a word wrong or make a grammar mistake that
  changes the meaning, correct me right away — briefly,
  one sentence, then continue the conversation
- keep track of small mistakes that don't change the
  meaning and tell me about them only at the end
- if I get stuck and don't know a word, don't give it to
  me right away: ask if I can describe it another way, and
  only then tell me
- ask me things, so that I do most of the talking

Today's conversation topic: [topic].
```

It returns a conversation pitched at the right difficulty. The line about small mistakes matters — if the model corrected everything on the spot, the conversation would collapse into a string of corrections and you'd stop talking.

### Scenarios instead of free-form chat

Free conversation “about anything” runs dry after a few minutes. It's better to play out concrete situations you'll actually run into: ordering at a restaurant, filing a complaint, a job interview, a support call, small talk at a conference. The model plays the other side, you train exactly the vocabulary you'll need.

```text
Let's act out a situation in [language]. You are [role, e.g.
a hotel receptionist dealing with an overbooked hotel]. I am
[role, e.g. a guest whose reservation isn't in the system].

Rules:
- stay in character and speak realistically, including not
  accommodating me on the first ask
- use expressions that would actually come up in this
  situation, not textbook sentences
- if I don't understand, I'll say “say that more simply”
  and you rephrase it, but stay in character

Once we're done: give me a text list of 10 useful phrases
from this situation that I didn't use and should know, and
5 mistakes I made, with corrections.
```

It returns a realistic practice run plus a glossary tailored to the situation at the end. Don't skimp on the phrases at the end — they're what sticks best, because you asked for them right after finding out you needed them.

### Pronunciation and fluency

For pronunciation, voice mode is only partly useful: the model reacts to what it recognized, so if it doesn't understand you, that's feedback in itself. Don't expect precise phonetic correction from it — a person or a specialized app still does that better. What it does deliver reliably is fluency: fifteen minutes of continuous talking a day makes a noticeable difference after a month, because it removes the pause where you'd otherwise assemble a sentence in your head.

## Limits worth knowing up front

Voice mode has a handful of hard limits. None of them are a reason not to use it — but all of them are a reason not to use it for certain things.

### Numbers, names, and exact figures

You won't retain a number you heard, and you'll retain a name you heard incorrectly. When something exact comes up in a conversation, say “write that down for me as text” and read it back at your desk. And in general: **voice mode is a poor fit for calculations and data** — not just because listening is hard, but because a language model computes by estimation. Numbers belong in a spreadsheet or a script you can rerun.

### No visuals

Voice doesn't show you a document, a chart, or a list. If you need to work with a source document, voice mode is the wrong tool — switch to a written chat, or have the material summarized for you beforehand and use voice only to think through the conclusions.

### The transcript gets things wrong

Speech recognition stumbles on proper names, technical terms, and foreign words — and it stumbles silently: the model answers based on what it thought it heard. When an answer doesn't fit, it isn't always a misunderstanding; often it's a bad transcription. Say “I meant [word], spelled like this” and move on.

### Privacy and your surroundings

People around you can hear a voice conversation. In an open office, on a train, or in a waiting room, you'll say a surprising number of names and internal details out loud in ten minutes. Speak in general terms (“client A,” “a project with a budget in the low hundreds of thousands”) and save sensitive matters for when you're alone. The same rule that applies across this whole site applies here too: **a voice conversation reaches the AI provider the same way written text does** — sensitive data belongs only in a paid account with a contractual data-protection agreement.

### Connection quality and offline

Conversation mode needs a connection. It doesn't work in the woods, on the subway, or in a garage. The backup is trivial: record a monologue on a voice recorder and process it once you're back online — the process is in the tip [dictate your thoughts, let AI turn them into text](/tipy/ai-diktat-ucesat).

### Fatigue and length

Listening and talking at the same time is more demanding than reading. After thirty to forty minutes, the quality of the conversation visibly drops — you start agreeing instead of thinking. Twenty minutes with a clear goal outperforms an hour of aimless chatting.

## Common mistakes

- **Not setting the mode at the start.** Without the instruction “keep it short, don't summarize, ask questions,” you'll spend the walk listening to your own thoughts read back to you. Thirty seconds of setup changes the whole conversation.
- **Answering briefly.** Voice mode pays off when you talk at length and without much order. Answer in one sentence and you get generic questions and generic answers back.
- **Taking advice instead of being questioned.** Advice from a model that doesn't know the context is usually worthless. The value is in questions that force you to say what you skipped.
- **Not closing with a summary.** A conversation with no record dissolves by evening. The last two minutes belong to a precisely specified summary, not a vague feeling that it was useful.
- **Using voice for things that should be written.** Text going out, numbers, code, long lists. Voice is for thinking, writing is for producing.
- **Forgetting who's listening.** Names and internal figures said out loud on a train are heard by the whole car — and they go to the service provider the same way written text does.

## The best tools

- **ChatGPT voice mode** — smooth conversation in the mobile app, handles interruptions well; the most common choice for both brainstorming and language practice.
- **Gemini Live** — Google's equivalent, well integrated into Android phones, so it launches the fastest.
- **Claude voice mode** — a good fit if you already run projects with persistent context in Claude and want to pick them up with voice instead of typing.
- **Earbuds with a mic** — the one piece of gear that genuinely changes the quality of a voice conversation outdoors; without them, street noise makes it hard for the model to hear you.
- **A voice recorder as a backup** — for places with no signal: record a monologue and process it later, and even without real-time answers it retains most of its value.
- **A notes app for summaries** — a place for the written summaries from your conversations to land, so you can write from them at your computer.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** twenty minutes of walking with a clear goal replaces roughly an hour of sitting over a blank page — especially for pieces where most of the work is sorting out your own thinking.
- **Reclaimed time:** commutes, walks, and driving turn into thinking time without costing you time anywhere else. Over a week, that adds up to hours.
- **Sanity:** the pressure of the blank screen disappears. You can talk in states where writing just doesn't happen, and a half-formed thought stops circling in your head once you've said it out loud once.
- **Quality:** being questioned exposes weaknesses in an argument before a reader or an audience does. The most valuable thing to bring home from a walk is often a list of claims you have no basis for.

## Pro tip

An advanced trick that turns voice mode into something more than a smart colleague: **have it quiz you on what you just came up with yourself.** At the end of a conversation, say “now switch roles — you're leadership, I'm presenting this to you in two days, and you have objections” and keep walking. A conversation where you have to defend your own fresh idea out loud, against someone difficult, reveals more in five minutes than two hours of polishing slides. A more systematic version is described in the tip [AI as your opponent](/tipy/ai-oponent).

And a closing rule: **a conversation never produces a commitment on its own, only material to work from.** Anything with real consequences — a sent email, a promised deadline, a decision announced to the team — goes through your own read-through at a computer first. Voice mode is great for figuring out what you want to do. Doing it is still on you.
