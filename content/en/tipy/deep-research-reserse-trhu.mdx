An ordinary AI chat answers from memory, in a few seconds. Deep research is something else: you ask a question, the tool plans out on its own what it needs to find, works through dozens of web sources, compares them, and returns a structured report with links showing where every claim comes from. It takes minutes instead of seconds — and replaces work a person would spend days on. **The key isn't that AI reads faster than you. It's that it finds sources you'd never have thought to look for.** The rest of this tip is about how to turn that into something a decision can actually rest on, instead of a nicely formatted mix of fact and invention.

## A typical scenario

Mark runs a small hardware-and-DIY-supplies company and is considering entering the Polish market. A proper investigation would mean a week of work: who's selling there, at what prices, through which channels, what the return policies look like, how big the market even is. In practice it would go differently — two hours of googling, three competitor websites, a gut-feel decision.

Instead, he submits a deep-research query with a precisely scoped question and goes to lunch. He comes back to an eight-page report: seven major players with positioning and price tiers, the three biggest e-commerce platforms in the country, a note on the requirements for selling into Poland, and a segment-size estimate with two different figures from two sources — which is itself useful information. Mark then spends an hour clicking through the citations behind the numbers he cares about. Two check out, one points to an old article, and one links to a page that doesn't actually contain the claimed figure. Even so: a week of work became an afternoon.

## How to do it

1. **Phrase the question as a brief, not a keyword.** “Competitors in Poland” is a bad prompt. A good brief answers three things: **exactly what you're trying to find out, for whom, and what decision it will inform.** For example: “Map the main online sellers of hand tools for home DIY use in Poland — positioning, price tiers, channels. This will inform a decision on whether to enter with our own mid-tier brand.” The difference in output quality is enormous.
2. **Say what the output should look like.** A competitor table, a list of risks, three scenarios, a half-page summary for leadership. If you don't specify a format, you'll get an essay — and rewrite it yourself anyway.
3. **Set boundaries.** A time window (“only sources from the last two years”), source language, geography, and what you don't care about. Without that, the research sprawls sideways.
4. **Let it run and do something else in the meantime.** Deep research takes minutes to run; it isn't a conversation, it's a task.
5. **Read critically and click the citations.** This is the part nobody does, and it's the part that matters most. **Go through every number the decision depends on and open its source.** Numbers are where hallucinations happen most often and are hardest to spot, because they look exactly right. Check the source's date too — two-year-old market data may be worthless. The method is in the tip on [verifying facts](/tipy/ai-overovani-faktu).
6. **Follow up on the gaps.** A report is the start of a conversation, not the end. “Where are the biggest uncertainties in this analysis?” and “what would have to be true for this conclusion to be wrong?” pull out more than another round of searching would.

## The best tools

- **[Claude](https://claude.ai)** — deep research with web search and citations on every claim; you can have the output converted straight into a table or a leadership summary.
- **Other major AI tools** — most offer a similar mode; they differ in depth and how they surface sources.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** days of gathering sources become minutes of runtime plus an hour of verification.
- **Breadth:** you find players, sources, and connections you'd have missed doing it by hand — especially in foreign-language markets.
- **Defensibility:** a report with links can be sent to colleagues and defended. “I read it somewhere” can't be.
- **Better questions:** the first pass of research mostly shows you what to ask the second time.

## Pro tip

Ask the same question twice, spaced apart and worded slightly differently — then compare. Claims that show up in both reports with the same sources are usually solid; claims that show up only once are candidates for verification. And watch out for sensitive data: if you're putting internal numbers, price lists, or client information into the brief, that belongs strictly on a **paid or business account with contractual data protection**, not a freely available chat — and anonymize what you can. The decision on entering a market, setting a price, or making an investment is then made by a person: **AI proposes, the person approves.**
