“What's for dinner?” sounds innocent, but it's one of the most expensive decisions of the day. It hits around half past four, when you're tired, hungry, and have no capacity left to plan — so you end up with delivery, or pasta for the third time that week, or a trip to the store for three things that turns into seven. **The fix isn't more willpower; it's moving the decision somewhere else: to Sunday, when things are calm, and ideally out of your head entirely.** A weekly menu and a shopping list sorted by store aisle are exactly the kind of repeated work a machine handles well and people don't enjoy.

## A typical scenario

Maria and James have two kids, seven and four, and both work. They shop three times a week on the way home, because no one knows in advance what's for dinner. The younger one won't eat fish, the older one hates bell peppers, and James is gluten-free due to celiac disease. Every week they toss out a fair bit of vegetables that never got used, plus a few yogurts past date. The evening debate over “so what should we make?” takes ten minutes and ends in a compromise nobody's happy with.

Now they have a Sunday routine. By six in the evening, the week's menu is ready: five dinners, two weekend lunches, one meal deliberately simple for Wednesday, when the older kid has an activity. Two evenings share ingredients on purpose, so they're not buying five different things for one-time use. Alongside it, a shopping list split into bakery, produce, dairy, meat, pantry, and household — in the order they walk through the store. Maria runs through it in two minutes, crosses off two meals that don't work for her that week, and shops once. Wasted food is down to scraps, and the evening debate is gone.

## How to do it

1. **Write the brief once, properly.** The only somewhat demanding part, and you do it exactly once: how many people and what ages, allergies and conditions (gluten-free, lactose-free), what anyone absolutely won't eat, how much time you have to cook on weekdays versus weekends, your rough weekly budget, and how often you want meat. The more specific this is, the less you'll need to rewrite the result.
2. **Save the brief as persistent context.** Don't retype it every week. Set up a project, upload it once, and AI knows it in every future conversation ([projects and persistent context](/tipy/ai-projekty-kontext)). This is family data, including health conditions — use a **paid account with contractual data protection** and replace kids' names with “older” and “younger.”
3. **Add what you already have at home.** The single most useful sentence in this whole process: “I have two chicken breasts and peas in the freezer, lentils, rice, and canned tomatoes in the pantry. Build the menu so those get used up.” This is where you save the most — planning starts from what you have, not from recipes you like. If you don't want to type it all out, photograph the open freezer and have the contents read from the picture.
4. **Ask for two outputs, not one.** First, a day-by-day menu with an estimated prep time. Second, a **shopping list sorted by category or store aisle**, with quantities merged — if onion shows up in three recipes, you want one line reading “onion, 5,” not three separate entries.
5. **Turn it into a scheduled task.** This is where the tip becomes a system. Set up a routine that fires every Sunday afternoon and prepares the menu and list on its own — the output is just waiting for you, no need to ask for it. It's the fourth level of working with AI, described in [the chapter on AI and automation](/prirucka/ai-a-automatizace).
6. **Cross things out — don't approve silently.** Remove or swap two meals from the draft each time, not because they're bad but because feedback is what tunes the routine. Add a sentence to the brief (“lentils at most once every two weeks,” “Friday should be something simple”) and next Sunday's draft lands closer to the mark.

## The best tools

- **[Claude](https://claude.ai)** — persistent context in a project, a scheduled Sunday routine, and the ability to read the freezer's contents from a photo; the shopping list can come back as a checkbox artifact ready for your phone.
- **A shared list on your phone (Google Keep, Notes, Todoist)** — where the list belongs, so both of you can see it and check things off as you go; whoever's near the store shops.
- **[Notion](https://notion.com)** — if you want to archive menus: a database of weeks with meal ratings works like a family cookbook that fills itself in.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** no more planning, repeat store runs, or daily decision-making. Conservatively, two hours a week — more for a bigger household.
- **Peace of mind:** decision fatigue at the worst moment of the day disappears. By 4:30 it's already decided — and you decided it, just on Sunday.
- **Money:** planning from what you already have, plus one big shop instead of four small ones, means significantly less wasted food and fewer impulse buys in the cart.
- **Quality of meals:** a weekly view keeps variety that daily improvising can't manage. Fish, legumes, and vegetables actually show up in the plan — almost never at 4:30 in the evening.

## Pro tip

Build a regular “leftovers” slot into the menu — one evening a week where nothing new gets cooked and you eat what's left over. Tell the routine explicitly (“plan a leftovers meal for Thursday, don't buy anything for it”) and pantry turnover improves on its own. And hold the line that applies everywhere on this site: **AI proposes, the person approves.** Let the menu generate itself, and the shopping list too — but don't give any tool the authority to order groceries and pay for them on your behalf. A human always sends the order, after looking at what's actually in the cart.
