You used to be able to spot machine translation from the first sentence — stiff word order, literal phrases, lost nuance, text that made sense but a native speaker would never have written it that way. With a good prompt, today's AI models handle it differently: they don't just translate words, they translate tone, formality, and context — who the text is for and why they're reading it.

## A typical scenario

A freelancer sends a proposal to a British client using a quick machine translation — the result reads like a textbook, formal where it should be relaxed and the other way around, and after the first two sentences the client gets the impression they're not dealing with a professional. Without this tip, the client responds coldly, the proposal doesn't feel trustworthy, and the collaboration gets off to a rocky start. With this tip, she gives AI the context — “formal, for a British client, construction industry” — and gets text that reads as if written by a native speaker familiar with the field, so the first impression lands right the first time.

It works for shorter messages too — one extra sentence of context often decides whether the text sounds professional or just translated.

## How to do it

1. Don't just tell AI “translate” — that request gets you a literal, impersonal translation.
2. Add context: who the text is for, how formal it should be, what industry you're in. For example: “Translate to English, formal, for a British construction-industry client.”
3. For technical text, specify whether technical terms should stay in English or be translated: “Translate for a technical audience, keep the industry terms in English.”
4. To check the result, use a back-translation — “translate this back into Czech for me” — and compare it to make sure the meaning hasn't shifted.
5. For important text, like contracts or official correspondence, have a native speaker or a colleague fluent in the language review the translation too.
6. For recurring types of text (proposals, invoices, email templates), save the prompt with its context and just reuse it next time — no need to reinvent it every time.

## The best tools

For everyday business communication, the first two tools are enough; a specialized translator makes sense for contracts and official documents.

- **ChatGPT / Claude / Gemini with described context** — handle tone, formality, and industry, not just the dictionary.
- **DeepL** — a specialized translator that keeps a very natural output for many language pairs even without a long prompt.
- **Google Translate** — a quick option for simple, informal text and getting the gist of a foreign-language text.
- **A native speaker or professional translator** — for contracts and official documents, where a mistake costs you your reputation.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** a faster path to a usable translation than hunting manually for the right phrases or consulting a dictionary.
- **Impression:** the text sounds natural, not translated — important on first contact with a client abroad, where the impression only forms once.
- **Accuracy:** a back-translation exposes where the meaning shifted before the text goes out.
- **Consistency:** with the same tone instructions, you get similarly styled translations across all your texts, even ones written by different people on the team.

## Pro tip

If you're translating sensitive correspondence — a contract, a client complaint, an internal message with names — consider stripping out personal details and numbers that aren't needed for the translation itself before pasting it into AI. The meaning and tone hold up even with anonymized placeholder names, and you can use the result just as well.
