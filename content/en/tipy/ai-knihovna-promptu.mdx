A good prompt is a tool, not a one-off note. Once you find a phrasing that reliably delivers a quality result — for meeting notes, a complaint response, or a contract summary — there's no point reinventing it every time you need it again. Tools don't get lost; they get stored in one place you can pull them from next time, the same way you don't make a new screwdriver for every screw.

## A typical scenario

A marketer reinvents her prompt for summarizing social media analytics every week, phrased slightly differently each time, with slightly different results, and never quite remembers what worked best last time. Without a prompt library: fifteen minutes tweaking the request just to get back to last week's quality, and often she still doesn't quite hit it. With a library, she just opens a note, copies the proven prompt, and drops in the current numbers — done in a minute, and the output quality is consistent every time.

Over time, a simple collection turns into a personal toolkit you can rely on the same way you rely on a favorite Excel template.

## How to do it

1. Set up a simple place to store prompts — a Notion page, a text document, a notes app, anything you always have access to.
2. For each prompt, note three things: what it's for, the prompt text itself, and a note on what could be improved next time.
3. Sort prompts by use case — emails, analysis, writing, document review — so you can navigate the library quickly.
4. Put your most frequently used prompts into your system's or keyboard's text snippets — a shortcut like “;notes” inserts the whole meeting-notes prompt in one go instead of digging through your notes.
5. Clean out the library periodically — update or delete prompts that stopped working well with a newer AI version.
6. When you spot an interesting prompt somewhere else — from a colleague or online — don't paste it straight into the conversation. Save it to the library first, so you still have it next time.

## The best tools

- **Your system's text snippets** (Text Replacement on Mac, text replacement on your phone) — a free built-in feature; you call up a prompt with a few characters.
- **Notion or a similar notes tool** — for a larger library with categories and search.
- **A custom project in ChatGPT or Claude with saved instructions** — the prompt is already part of the environment, so you don't even need to copy it.
- **A shared document** (Google Docs) — if you're building a prompt library as a team and want to share it.
- **A plain notepad file** — if you prefer minimalism, even a plain text file with headings works reliably.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** no more re-phrasing the same request — ten minutes of tweaking becomes one click.
- **Consistency:** the same prompt produces similarly good results, so the tone of your output stays consistent from week to week.
- **Sanity:** you don't have to remember the exact wording that worked back then, or dig it up in your conversation history.
- **Sharing:** you can hand proven prompts to a colleague or a new team member without having to explain them all over again.

## Pro tip

Don't put specific sensitive data, like client names or contract numbers, directly into the prompts in your library — write them as a template with a generic placeholder you fill in at the time of use. That way the prompt library can be safely shared with colleagues without holding real company data, and it stays usable even after the client or project changes.
