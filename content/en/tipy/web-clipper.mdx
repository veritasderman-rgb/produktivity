“That article was great, too bad I can't find it now.” You've probably heard yourself say this — a bookmark gets lost among hundreds of others, or you leave a tab open in the browser until you close it, unread, weeks later. A web clipper solves this problem once and for all: the article gets saved to a library where you can actually find it, even six months later.

## A typical scenario

Eva, a marketer, runs across five interesting articles during the day — one about industry trends, another with a specific number she'll need for a presentation. Without a clipper, she saves them as bookmarks in a “Read later” folder that already has over a hundred unnamed, unsorted entries. A month later, when she needs to find the one with the number, she goes through them one by one and eventually gives up.

With a web clipper, she presses <kbd>Ctrl</kbd> + <kbd>Shift</kbd> + <kbd>K</kbd> while reading, adds the tag “marketing,” and the article disappears into her library with the text and images saved — regardless of whether the page later vanishes from the internet. A month later she finds it through full-text search on a keyword from the text, not by the bookmark's name that nobody remembers.

## How to do it

1. Install a browser extension — **Notion Web Clipper** is one option, others include Pocket, Instapaper, or Obsidian Web Clipper, depending on where you want to save your content.
2. Set up a keyboard shortcut for quick saving — e.g. <kbd>Ctrl</kbd> + <kbd>Shift</kbd> + <kbd>K</kbd> — so you don't have to reach for the mouse and hunt for the extension icon every time.
3. Save everything into a single database or “To read” list with a clear status: new → read → archive. Without this structure, you'll end up with the same unsearchable pile, just in a different app.
4. Add at least one tag or category when saving — it makes later searching much easier than relying on full text alone, especially for shorter articles where the keyword might not appear verbatim.
5. Most clippers can save a selected passage instead of the whole page — handy when you only care about one paragraph or quote, not the entire long article.

## The best tools

- **Notion Web Clipper** — saves directly into a Notion database, a good choice if you already keep notes in Notion and want everything in one place.
- **Pocket** — a simple tool focused purely on read-later, including offline mode and a clean, ad-free reading view.
- **Instapaper** — similar to Pocket, plus a built-in reading-time estimate and the option to listen to articles as audio.
- **Obsidian Web Clipper** — for people who keep notes in Obsidian; a saved article becomes part of a linked note network instead of an isolated item.

## What you get out of it

- **Time:** tens of seconds saved when looking for an article later add up to ten or more minutes a day for a regular reader.
- **Less lost content:** pages change or disappear over time — a saved copy of the text stays accessible even after the original is gone from the web.
- **Actual reading instead of hoarding:** a database with a status forces you to come back to saved articles instead of letting the list grow forever like a pile of bookmarks.
- **Better visibility into your own sources:** tags and categories gradually build a personal library you can search by topic, not just by chance.

## Pro tip

Once a week, set aside ten minutes for your database and go through it: anything worth keeping, turn into your own notes or a summary; archive or delete the rest without regret. Saving without reading is just digital hoarding, and without regular cleanup it ends up just as unsearchable as old bookmarks.
