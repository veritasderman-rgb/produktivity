Do you know the feeling of having a head full of tasks and no idea where to start? Or lying awake at night while your brain reminds you of everything you haven't finished? You're not alone — and there is a fix.

## Why you need a system

Think of your brain as a computer's processor. Open too many programs at once and it slows down and overheats. That's exactly what happens when you try to hold every task, deadline and idea in your head. Without a system you forget important things, deadlines stress you out, work piles up — and in the evening you can't “switch off.”

### 1. External storage (the inbox)

Pick **one** tool for capturing tasks — paper or digital — and write down **everything**, including things that seem trivial. Then go through the storage regularly and sort it.

> **From experience:** For a long time I used a combination of a paper planner, a digital calendar and sticky notes on my monitor. The result? Chaos. Today everything lives in one system and I finally have order.

### 2. The Eisenhower matrix

### 3. Regular review

A system is only as good as how often you update it:

How to work with the matrix in practice:

1. **Important + urgent = do it now.** Crises, deadlines, urgent problems. You can't dodge this quadrant — but you can shrink it.
2. **Important + not urgent = schedule it.** Strategic projects, personal growth, preventing problems. This is where real progress happens — and without scheduling, this quadrant always loses to whatever is urgent.
3. **Not important + urgent = delegate it.** Some meetings, some emails, interruptions from colleagues. Urgency is not the same thing as importance.
4. **Not important + not urgent = eliminate it.** Procrastination, pointless meetings, social media during work hours. Cut it without guilt.

## Getting started: the first steps

1. **Clear your head.** Take paper or a digital note and write down absolutely everything you're carrying around — tasks, ideas, projects, commitments, things that stress you out. Don't stop until your head is empty; typically that's 50–150 items, and the relief is physical.
2. **Build your system.** Pick a tool — digital (Todoist, Things, Microsoft To Do), analog (bullet journal, planner), or a combination. Then set up the basic categories: Projects, Personal, Work, Someday/Maybe.
3. **Put routines in place.** Morning planning (10 minutes), evening review (5 minutes), weekly overview (30 minutes). Routines are what turn a list into a system.

## Common obstacles

**“I don't have time to get organized.”** Start with 5 minutes a day. A simple system you actually use beats a perfect one that's too complicated.

**“I forget to check my lists.”** Attach the check to an existing daily routine — your morning coffee, for instance.

**“I have too many tasks.”** The important/urgent matrix plus learning to say no.

## How you'll know it's working

- Less stress at bedtime.
- Fewer forgotten tasks.
- More projects finished.
- A better overview of your own commitments.
- More time for what matters — because the system holds the overview, not your head.

There's no such thing as a perfect system — only a system that works **for you**. In the chapters ahead we'll look at specific methods (GTD, Inbox Zero, time blocking and more) as well as the tools that will help you digitize and automate your system.
