Most people don't have too few tools — they have too many, badly connected. Three note-taking apps, two task managers, four places files end up in, and five channels work arrives through. Which is why the road to a system starts, paradoxically, with cutting rather than installing.

## Step 1: Audit where you are now

Before you pick anything, take an honest inventory. Write down:

1. **Every installed app** — on your computer and your phone, including the ones you forgot about.
2. **Every online service you use** — everything you log into, even if it's once a quarter.
3. **Every place data lives** — cloud, local drive, email attachments, shared folders, USB sticks.
4. **Every communication channel** — email, chat, SMS, social media, project tools.

### Rating what you actually use

For each item on the list, note three things:

- **Frequency** — daily / weekly / monthly / rarely.
- **Real value** — indispensable / useful / nice to have / pointless.
- **Overlap** — unique function / partial overlap / complete duplicate.

That simple table does most of the work for you. Anything you rarely use, that adds nothing and fully duplicates something else, is a candidate for removal. No deliberation needed.

> **From experience:** on my first audit I found four note-taking apps. One held work notes, another recipes, a third an unfinished project from two years ago, and the fourth nothing at all. One won and the rest got exported to an archive — and I've known where to look for things ever since.

### Spotting the problems

An audit almost always surfaces three kinds of problems:

- **Duplicate tools** — several note apps, competing task managers, overlapping communication channels. The result: you never know where a given piece of information lives.
- **Unused tools** — forgotten subscriptions, stale data, dead projects. The result: you're paying for distraction.
- **Missing capabilities** — gaps in your workflow, needs nobody solved, improvised workarounds. The result: the most important things live in your head or in your inbox.

**Must-have** — without these five, don't take the tool:

- available across platforms (desktop and phone, ideally web too),
- cloud synchronization,
- data export to an open format,
- active development (when did the last update ship?),
- reliability — a tool that loses data once a month is worse than paper.

**Nice-to-have** — pleasant, but not decisive: offline mode, an API and integrations, a lively user community, plugin extensibility, an interface in your own language.

On top of that, add criteria specific to what you want the tool for:

- **Productivity:** speed, keyboard shortcuts, automation, templates. You will not keep using a tool where creating a task takes ten seconds.
- **Collaboration:** sharing, comments, version history, permissions and roles.
- **Security:** encryption, two-factor authentication, privacy compliance, backups. Always work through this when company or client data will go into the tool — and ask where the service physically stores that data.

## Step 3: The minimum effective set

Most people need **four tools**:

That's the necessary minimum. Once four tools cover tasks, knowledge, time and files, you have a working system — everything else is an add-on.

**Extend the stack** only when you hit a genuine obstacle:

- **Communication** — team chat, video calls, an email client.
- **Specialized tools for your profession** — design, development, project management.
- **Automation** — Zapier, IFTTT, Make, or your own scripts.

The golden rule: add a new tool only once a specific pain has existed for at least a month. Not sooner.

## Step 4: Connecting the tools

Tools without integration mean retyping things by hand — and retyping by hand means you'll abandon the system sooner or later. There are three basic connection strategies:

- **Hub and spoke** — one central tool (Notion, say) with specialized services attached around it. The clearest option, because you always know where to start looking.
- **Pipeline** — data flows in one direction: email → task manager → calendar. Excellent for processing incoming work.
- **Mesh** — everything connected to everything, with shared data; a fully integrated Google Workspace, for instance. The most powerful, and the hardest to debug.

<Flow title="Three levels of integration — most stable first" steps={[

Always start from the top. Native integrations are boring and work for years; custom scripts are fun and break with the first API update.

**Checklist before you go live:**

- back up your existing data — always, even when it seems like there's nothing at stake,
- run a test on one small project, not on your whole workload,
- document how things work — even a few lines about what goes where,
- train the people affected (for a team rollout).

**Checklist after you go live:**

- monitor actual usage — is it being used, or just sitting there?
- collect feedback from yourself and from the team,
- adjust as reality demands, not as the original plan demands,
- review regularly, ideally monthly for the first six months.

Remember: the goal isn't to own the most tools, it's to have an effective system that helps you get more done. Less really is more — as long as that less works well and is properly connected.
